# SANE Exam Audit Protocols

## Examiner Qualifications Audit

- SANE-A (adult) vs. SANE-P (pediatric) certification?
- Hours of specialized training completed?
- Supervised clinical experience documented?
- Current certification status and renewal record?
- **Challenge Point:** Protocols vary by hospital. Examiner may not be fully trained despite performing exam.

## Timing Analysis — SANE Exam Windows

| Time Window | Expected Findings | Significance |
|-------------|-------------------|--------------|
| 0–24 hours | Acute injuries most visible; semen recovery possible | Highest diagnostic window |
| 24–72 hours | Injuries may be healing; semen becomes harder to recover | Moderate diagnostic window |
| 72+ hours | Healing progressing; semen unlikely unless motility test positive | Limited diagnostic window |
| >7 days | Injuries largely healed; DNA unlikely unless in deep tissues | Minimal acute findings |

**Key Points:**
- When was exam performed relative to alleged assault?
- If >72 hours: acute findings increasingly unlikely
- If >5–7 days: injury healing obscures initial trauma
- **Challenge Point:** Delayed exams are presented as normal but limit evidentiary value.

## Documentation of Injuries (or Lack Thereof)

- Were injuries **actually found**?
- CRITICAL: Normal/no injury findings are common in confirmed abuse cases
- Research by Adams, Heger, Berenson: 60–90% of children with confirmed abuse show no anogenital injury
- Were findings **photographed**? Chain of custody of images?
- Were injuries documented in writing with anatomical descriptors?
- **Challenge Point:** "No injury" does not equal "no assault."

## Colposcope Findings & Interpretation

- Colposcope magnification 6–40x used?
- Specific findings documented (fissures, lacerations, scars, friability)?
- Who interpreted findings? (Examiner vs. radiologist vs. protocol)

### Understanding Normal Developmental Variation in Anogenital Anatomy

- Hymenal transections vs. hymenal notches (notches common, not diagnostic)
- Posterior fourchette variations
- Anal fissures (may be constipation-related)
- **Challenge Point:** Misinterpretation of normal anatomy as abuse is documented risk.

## Evidence Collection Technique Standards

- Were protocols followed for swabbing (location, pressure, moisture)?
- Proper specimen labeling and chain of custody?
- Contamination risk during collection (examiner gloves, equipment)?
- Samples frozen/stored properly?
- **Challenge Point:** Improper technique compromises DNA validity.

## Toxicology Screening

- Blood and/or urine drug screen performed?
- If victim alleges incapacity (date rape): toxicology critical
- Absence of drug screen when incapacity is core element is weakness
- **Challenge Point:** Prosecution may not obtain toxicology; absence is defense advantage.

## Patient History Documentation vs. Forensic Findings Consistency

- Did examiner document victim's account of assault (time, location, acts)?
- Does SANE documentation match victim's statement to police?
- Inconsistencies between "what victim said" and "what was found" are red flags
- **Challenge Point:** Discrepancies undermine cohesive narrative.

## Injury Healing Timeline Analysis

- If injuries are documented: when would they heal?
- Genital lacerations: typically 3–10 days
- Bruising: color progression allows rough dating (not precise)
- Scarring: develops over weeks
- **Challenge Point:** Medical expert may opine on healing timeline to challenge delay theories.

## Alternative Explanations for Physical Findings

- Consensual intercourse causes similar minor injuries
- Prior sexual activity, gynecological conditions, sexual practices
- Menstruation, hormonal changes, medical conditions
- Infection, inflammation from non-sexual causes
- **Challenge Point:** Physical findings alone insufficient for rape diagnosis.
