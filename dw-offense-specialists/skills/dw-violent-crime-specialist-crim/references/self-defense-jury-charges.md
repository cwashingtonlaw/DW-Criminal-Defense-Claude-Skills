# Self-Defense and Defense-of-Others Jury Charges — Louisiana

**ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT — PRIVILEGED & CONFIDENTIAL — PREPARED IN ANTICIPATION OF LITIGATION**

This reference compiles the controlling Louisiana self-defense statutes, the doctrinal lines defense counsel must police at the charge conference, and pattern jury-charge language for use by `dw-jury-instructions-builder-crim`. Invoke this file whenever a violent-crime case has any plausible self-defense, defense-of-others, defense-of-property, or aggressor/withdrawal issue.

The single most important doctrinal point: **Louisiana imposes NO duty to retreat.** La. R.S. 14:20(C) (the so-called "stand your ground" provision) eliminates any retreat requirement before using deadly force in lawful self-defense, and the trial court must charge that fact when self-defense is at issue. Any pattern instruction that even implies a retreat duty is reversible error and must be objected to on the record.

---

## 1. Statutory Framework

### La. R.S. 14:18 — Justification; general

Conduct that would otherwise be criminal is justifiable under the enumerated subsections, including reasonable self-defense, defense of others, and defense of property. La. R.S. 14:18 is the umbrella that the more specific sections (14:19, 14:20, 14:21, 14:22) operationalize. The defendant carries the burden of producing some evidence of justification; once produced, the State must disprove justification beyond a reasonable doubt.

### La. R.S. 14:19 — Use of force or violence in defense (non-deadly force)

Use of force or violence (short of deadly force) is justifiable when:
- Committed for the purpose of preventing a forcible offense against the person or a forcible offense or trespass against property in a person's lawful possession; AND
- The force used is reasonable and apparently necessary to prevent the offense.

There is no retreat requirement under La. R.S. 14:19(D). When non-deadly force is at issue, the defense should request a charge mirroring the statutory text, expressly including the no-retreat clause.

### La. R.S. 14:20 — Justifiable homicide (deadly force)

Deadly force in self-defense is justifiable in five enumerated circumstances:

1. La. R.S. 14:20(A)(1) — When committed in self-defense by one who reasonably believes that he is in imminent danger of losing his life or receiving great bodily harm AND that the killing is necessary to save himself from that danger.
2. La. R.S. 14:20(A)(2) — When committed for the purpose of preventing a violent or forcible felony involving danger to life or great bodily harm by one who reasonably believes that such an offense is about to be committed and that such action is necessary for its prevention.
3. La. R.S. 14:20(A)(3) — When committed against a person who has unlawfully entered or is attempting to enter the dwelling, place of business, or motor vehicle of the person committing the homicide, and who reasonably believes that the use of deadly force is necessary to prevent the entry or to compel the intruder to leave (the "castle" provision, with its statutory presumption of reasonableness).
4. La. R.S. 14:20(A)(4) — When committed by a person lawfully inside a dwelling, place of business, or motor vehicle, against a person who is attempting to make an unlawful entry or who has made an unlawful entry, and who reasonably believes that the use of deadly force is necessary to prevent the entry or to compel the intruder to leave.

La. R.S. 14:20(B) creates a **presumption of reasonable belief** when the homicide occurs against an unlawful intruder into a dwelling, business, or vehicle. The defense should request that the presumption be charged in the jury's own words.

La. R.S. 14:20(C) — **NO DUTY TO RETREAT.** A person who is not engaged in unlawful activity and who is in a place where he or she has a right to be shall have NO DUTY to retreat before using deadly force, and may stand his or her ground and meet force with force. This subsection must be charged verbatim in any homicide self-defense case.

La. R.S. 14:20(D) bars the trier of fact from considering the possibility of retreat as a factor in determining whether the defendant had a reasonable belief that deadly force was necessary. Any prosecutor argument inviting the jury to weigh retreat is objectionable; preserve the issue.

### La. R.S. 14:21 — Aggressor doctrine

A person who is the aggressor or who brings on a difficulty cannot claim the right of self-defense unless he withdraws from the conflict in good faith and in such a manner that his adversary knows or should know that he desires to withdraw and discontinue the conflict. The "withdrawal" prong is litigation-rich; the State will lean on minor escalation to convert the defendant into the aggressor, and the defense must police the line.

### La. R.S. 14:22 — Defense of others

The use of force or violence (including deadly force) for the protection of others is justifiable when the actor reasonably believes the person attacked would have been justified in using such force in self-defense. The defendant steps into the shoes of the third party. Mistaken-but-reasonable belief is sufficient — actual peril is not required.

## 2. Pattern Jury-Charge Language

The Louisiana pattern jury instructions (LPJI Crim. § 7.01 et seq.) provide a starting point, but defense counsel should tailor the charge to the statutory subsection actually in play. Submit proposed charges in writing under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 807.

### 2.1 Self-Defense — Deadly Force (homicide)

> "The defendant has raised the defense of self-defense. A homicide is justifiable when committed in self-defense by one who reasonably believes that he is in imminent danger of losing his life or receiving great bodily harm and that the killing is necessary to save himself from that danger.
>
> The State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the homicide was not committed in self-defense. If you have a reasonable doubt as to whether the defendant acted in self-defense, you must find the defendant not guilty.
>
> The defendant had NO DUTY to retreat before using deadly force. A person who is not engaged in unlawful activity and who is in a place where he or she has a right to be has no duty to retreat and may stand his or her ground and meet force with force. You may not consider the possibility of retreat as a factor in determining whether the defendant had a reasonable belief that the use of deadly force was necessary."

### 2.2 Self-Defense — Non-Deadly Force

> "The use of force or violence upon the person of another is justifiable when committed for the purpose of preventing a forcible offense against the person, provided that the force used must be reasonable and apparently necessary to prevent the offense. The defendant has no duty to retreat before using non-deadly force in self-defense. The State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not act in lawful self-defense."

### 2.3 Defense of Others

> "A homicide is also justifiable when committed in defense of another person against whom the defendant reasonably believed a forcible felony or unlawful attack was being or was about to be committed, if the person defended would have been justified in using such force in his own defense. The defendant's belief must be reasonable, but it does not have to be correct — a reasonable mistake of fact does not defeat the defense."

### 2.4 Castle/Vehicle/Business Presumption

> "When a homicide is committed against a person who has unlawfully entered or is attempting to unlawfully enter the defendant's dwelling, place of business, or motor vehicle, the law presumes that the defendant had a reasonable belief that the use of deadly force was necessary. The State bears the burden of overcoming that presumption beyond a reasonable doubt."

### 2.5 Aggressor Doctrine — Withdrawal

> "A person who is the aggressor or who brings on a difficulty cannot claim the right of self-defense unless he withdraws from the conflict in good faith and communicates that withdrawal to his adversary. If you find that the defendant was not the aggressor, or that the defendant was the aggressor but withdrew in good faith and his adversary knew or should have known of the withdrawal, then the defendant retains the right of self-defense."

## 3. Aggressor-Doctrine Litigation

The State's most common path to defeat self-defense is to brand the defendant the aggressor. Defense counsel must:

- **Identify the inception point.** Who threw the first punch, brandished the first weapon, made the first verbal threat that crossed into a forcible offense? Mere words are not enough to make a person an aggressor under settled Louisiana doctrine — confirm a current Louisiana case on the "mere words insufficient" rule before filing.
- **Police the "bringing on a difficulty" theory.** The State will argue that the defendant created the conditions for the violence (e.g., showing up to a known dispute, returning to the scene). Push back: bringing-on requires conduct that itself constitutes a forcible offense or threat of one.
- **Document withdrawal.** Verbal disengagement, physical retreat, weapon being lowered, hands up, distance opened — anything that signaled the desire to end the conflict. The withdrawal need only be communicated in a way the adversary "knew or should have known."
- **Resist State efforts to charge the aggressor doctrine without an evidentiary basis.** The aggressor instruction is not a default; the State must point to evidence supporting it before the court charges it.

## 4. Reasonableness — Subjective and Objective Components

Louisiana applies a hybrid reasonableness test. The defendant's belief must be:
- **Subjectively held** (the defendant actually believed deadly force was necessary), AND
- **Objectively reasonable** (a reasonable person in the defendant's position, with the same knowledge and circumstances, would have believed the same).

Defense should request a charge that names both components and reminds the jury that perfect judgment is not the standard. Combat conditions, prior threats, the disparity of force (size, age, weapon possession), and the defendant's knowledge of the victim's history of violence are all relevant. La. C.E. Art. 404(A)(2)(a) makes the alleged victim's character for violence (and prior acts known to the defendant) admissible when self-defense is at issue; *State v. Lee*, 331 So.2d 455 (La. 1976), is one of the foundational cases on victim-character admissibility under what is now Art. 404(A)(2)(a).

Proportionality is not a separate element — Louisiana's statute speaks of "necessary" force, not "proportionate" force. Object to any State argument or jury charge that imports a strict proportionality requirement.

---

## 5. Burden of Proof

After the defendant produces some evidence of self-defense (a low burden — even the State's own evidence may suffice), the burden of disproving self-defense beyond a reasonable doubt rests with the State. The defense should request a "shifting burden" charge that explicitly tells the jury:

> "The defendant does not have to prove self-defense. Once self-defense becomes an issue in the case, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did NOT act in self-defense. If the State fails to disprove self-defense beyond a reasonable doubt, you must find the defendant not guilty."

Object to any pattern instruction that places a persuasion burden on the defendant.
