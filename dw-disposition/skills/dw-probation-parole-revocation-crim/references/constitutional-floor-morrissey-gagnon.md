# The Constitutional Floor — Morrissey / Gagnon Due Process

Revocation is not a criminal prosecution, but it deprives the client of conditional liberty, so the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause sets a floor under every revocation proceeding. *Morrissey v. Brewer*, 408 U.S. 471 (1972) (parole); *Gagnon v. Scarpelli*, 411 U.S. 778 (1973) (extending Morrissey to probation). Every hearing-prep task in this skill maps back to one of these rights.

## The two-stage structure

| Stage | Purpose | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Preliminary (probable-cause) hearing** | Independent determination that there is probable cause to believe a violation occurred | Promptly after arrest, at or reasonably near the place of the alleged violation (*Morrissey*; implemented for parole by La. R.S. 15:574.7's prerevocation hearing) |
| **Final revocation hearing** | Full determination of violation and disposition | Within a reasonable time after custody begins (Louisiana probation: in-custody defendant brought before the court within 30 days, La. C.Cr.P. art. 900) |

## The six Morrissey minimum rights — defense attack map

| # | Right | What to demand / attack |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Written notice of the claimed violations** | Compare the notice against the evidence actually offered at the hearing. A violation found on conduct not noticed is a due-process violation. Vague notice ("violated condition 4") that does not say *what conduct* is challengeable. |
| 2 | **Disclosure of the evidence against the client** | Demand the violation report, chronos, lab results, and any witness statements before the hearing. Surprise evidence → object and request a continuance on the record. |
| 3 | **Opportunity to be heard in person and to present witnesses and documentary evidence** | Subpoena defense witnesses; proffer mitigation documents; object if the court limits presentation. |
| 4 | **Right to confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses, unless the hearing officer specifically finds good cause for not allowing confrontation** | The workhorse objection. When the State proves the violation through the supervising officer reading someone else's report, demand a **specific, on-the-record good-cause finding** before the hearsay substitutes for the live witness. No finding = preserved error. See Module A. |
| 5 | **A neutral and detached hearing body** | For parole, panel members with prior investigative involvement; for probation, a judge who has prejudged the violation on the record. Rare but preserve it when it appears. |
| 6 | **A written statement by the factfinder as to the evidence relied on and reasons for revoking** | Request written (or transcribed oral) findings at the close of every adverse hearing. Thin or missing findings are a lead writ/judicial-review issue and boxes in the State's theory. |

## Counsel

*Gagnon* holds that appointed counsel at revocation is determined case-by-case (presumptively required where the violation is contested or the mitigation case is complex). Louisiana practice supplies more: counsel appears at Art. 900 probation hearings as a matter of course, and for parole revocation the parolee has the right to be represented by retained or appointed counsel (La. R.S. 15:574.9, referencing appointment under La. R.S. 15:179 [VERIFY CITATION — confirm current cross-reference for appointment of counsel at parole revocation]). Never let a client waive the hearing or admit a violation without counsel review — under La. R.S. 15:574.9 a written waiver of the parole revocation hearing constitutes an admission and results in revocation.

## Companion doctrines

- **Failure-to-pay violations:** *Bearden v. Georgia*, 461 U.S. 660 (1983) — the court may not revoke for failure to pay a fine, fee, or restitution without inquiring into ability to pay and finding the failure willful or that alternatives are inadequate. Always demand the *Bearden* inquiry on the record for any money-based violation.
- **Exclusionary rule:** *Pennsylvania Bd. of Probation & Parole v. Scott*, 524 U.S. 357 (1998) — the federal exclusionary rule does not apply at parole revocation hearings. Do not build the defense around suppression at the revocation hearing itself; illegally seized evidence usually comes in. The suppression fight belongs to the new criminal case (Module F), and egregious/harassing searches can still be argued as a due-process matter [VERIFY — Louisiana jurisprudence on state-law exclusion at revocation before relying].
- **Standard of proof:** Revocation does not require proof beyond a reasonable doubt; the judge (or committee) must be satisfied by reliable evidence that a condition was violated. Frame factual defenses accordingly — attack reliability and sourcing, not just sufficiency.
