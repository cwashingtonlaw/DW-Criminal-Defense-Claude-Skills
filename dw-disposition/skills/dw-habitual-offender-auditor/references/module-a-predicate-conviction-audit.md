# MODULE A — Predicate Conviction Audit

This module systematically verifies every element the State must prove for each predicate conviction. The State bears the burden of proving each predicate conviction beyond a reasonable doubt at the habitual offender hearing. *State v. Carlos*, 98 So.3d 727 (La. App. 4th Cir. 2012); *State v. Payton*, 810 So.2d 1127 (La. 2002).

## The Five Elements of a Valid Predicate

For each predicate conviction, the State must prove all five elements. Failure on any single element defeats that predicate.

**Element 1 — Identity:**
The State must prove the defendant before the court is the same person who was convicted in the predicate case. Methods of proof include:
- Testimony of a witness who was present at the prior proceeding
- Expert testimony comparing fingerprints from the prior conviction with the defendant's known fingerprints
- Certified copies of fingerprint cards from the prior conviction matched to the defendant
- Photographs from prior booking records
- Defendant's own testimony or admissions
- Pen packet or Department of Corrections records linking the defendant to the prior conviction

*State v. Curtis*, 338 So.2d 662 (La. 1976) — fingerprint evidence is the preferred method of identity proof for predicate convictions.

**Audit checkpoint:** Does the State's evidence actually establish that *this* defendant is the person convicted in the predicate case? Are there name variations, aliases, or common names that create ambiguity? Were fingerprints compared, or is the State relying solely on name/DOB matching?

**Element 2 — Valid Conviction:**
The predicate must be a valid, final conviction — not an arrest, a charge, or a dismissed case. The State must produce:
- A certified copy of the bill of information or indictment
- Minute entries or court records reflecting the conviction (guilty plea or verdict)
- Commitment order or sentencing transcript

*State v. Baker*, 230 So.3d 207 (La. App. 4th Cir. 2017) — predicate conviction must be final; pending appeal may defeat finality depending on the circumstances.

**Audit checkpoint:** Is each predicate supported by a certified copy of the conviction? Is the conviction final (no pending appeal, no post-conviction relief granted)? Has the conviction been reversed, vacated, or set aside?

**Element 3 — Felony Status:**
Only felony convictions may serve as predicates under La. R.S. 15:529.1. The State must prove the predicate offense was a felony at the time of conviction.

**Audit checkpoint:** Was the offense a felony when the defendant was convicted? Louisiana has reclassified some offenses from felonies to misdemeanors over the years (e.g., certain drug possession offenses after the 2017 Justice Reinvestment reforms). If the offense has been reclassified, determine whether the reclassification applies retroactively to defeat the predicate.

**Element 4 — Boykinization (for guilty plea predicates):**
If the predicate conviction was obtained by guilty plea, the State must demonstrate the plea was constitutionally valid under *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969). This is the most frequently challenged element and is analyzed in depth in Module B.

**Element 5 — Sequence & Timing:**
Each subsequent offense must have been committed after the conviction for the prior offense became final. This sequence requirement is analyzed in Module C. Additionally, each predicate must fall within the applicable cleansing period under La. R.S. 15:529.1(C), analyzed in Module D.

## Predicate Audit Severity Rating

After auditing each predicate, assign a severity rating:

| Rating | Meaning | Attorney Action |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| **FATAL DEFICIENCY** | The deficiency defeats this predicate as a matter of law — the State cannot cure it with the existing record | File challenge motion; argue at hearing; this predicate should be stricken |
| **SIGNIFICANT DEFICIENCY** | The deficiency creates a strong challenge — the State may or may not be able to cure it, depending on what additional records exist | File challenge motion; prepare cross-examination; explore whether the State can produce additional documentation |
| **MODERATE DEFICIENCY** | The deficiency raises a legitimate legal question but may be overcome by the State with additional proof | Flag for hearing; prepare arguments; assess whether the deficiency is worth challenging given the overall strategy |
| **MINOR DEFICIENCY** | A technical or documentation gap that is unlikely to defeat the predicate alone but contributes to the overall picture of unreliable proof | Note for cumulative argument; useful for plea negotiation leverage |
| **NO DEFICIENCY** | The predicate is properly documented and appears constitutionally sound | Acknowledge; focus resources on other predicates |
