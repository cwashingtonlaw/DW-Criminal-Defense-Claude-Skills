# Harmless-Error Framework — Louisiana Direct Appeal

After establishing that the trial court erred, the appellant must address harmless-error analysis. The harmless-error inquiry asks whether the established error warrants reversal — or whether the conviction stands despite the error. Two harmless-error standards govern Louisiana criminal appeals; a narrow third category (structural error) requires automatic reversal without harmless-error analysis at all.

This reference catalogs the three frameworks, identifies which standard applies to which kind of error, and supplies per-issue templates for the Prejudice / Harmless-Error subsection (D.6) of each Argument section in the appellate brief.

---

## 1. The Three Frameworks at a Glance

| Framework | Applies to | Burden | Result if not met |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structural error** | Narrow categories — defects affecting the framework of the trial | NO harmless-error analysis | Automatic reversal |
| ***Chapman* harmless-error** | Federal constitutional errors (4th/5th/6th Amendment, Confrontation, due process) | State must prove harmless beyond a reasonable doubt | Reversal |
| **Art. 921 harmless-error** | Non-constitutional errors (most evidentiary rulings, procedural irregularities) | Error must "affect substantial rights" | Reversal |

**Drafting rule:** For every non-structural assignment of error, the brief's Prejudice / Harmless-Error subsection (D.6) must:
1. Identify which framework applies (constitutional Chapman vs. non-constitutional Art. 921).
2. State the burden allocation.
3. Walk through the record to show why the error was NOT harmless.

For structural errors, the brief states that the error is structural, cites the controlling authority, and states that no harmless-error analysis applies — the conviction must be reversed automatically.

---

## 2. Structural Errors — Automatic Reversal

Structural errors are defects that "affect[] the framework within which the trial proceeds" and that "infect the entire trial process." *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 309-10 (1991). These errors are not amenable to harmless-error analysis because the harm cannot be measured.

The U.S. Supreme Court has identified a narrow set of structural errors. The Louisiana Supreme Court generally tracks the federal categories.

### 2.1 Federally recognized structural errors

| Error | Authority |
|---|---|
| Total deprivation of right to counsel | *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963); *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648 (1984) |
| Biased trial judge | *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927) |
| Race-based exclusion of grand jurors | *Vasquez v. Hillery*, 474 U.S. 254 (1986) |
| Denial of right to self-representation | *McKaskle v. Wiggins*, 465 U.S. 168 (1984); *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975) |
| Denial of public trial | *Waller v. Georgia*, 467 U.S. 39 (1984) |
| **Defective reasonable-doubt instruction** | *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993) |
| **Counsel conceding guilt over defendant's objection** | *McCoy v. Louisiana*, 584 U.S. 414 (2018) |
| Race-based peremptory challenges | *Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986); *Vasquez v. Hillery* |
| **Non-unanimous jury verdict** (felony) | *Ramos v. Louisiana*, 590 U.S. 83 (2020) |
| Erroneous denial of motion to dismiss for vindictive prosecution | *[narrow circumstances]* |

### 2.2 Drafting language for structural-error brief sections

**Template — D.6 Prejudice (structural error):**

```
Because [identify error] is a structural error, harmless-error analysis
does not apply. [Authority — e.g., Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275
(1993); McCoy v. Louisiana, 584 U.S. 414 (2018); Ramos v. Louisiana, 590
U.S. 83 (2020)]. Structural errors "affect[] the framework within which
the trial proceeds" and "infect the entire trial process." Arizona v.
Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279, 309-10 (1991). They are not amenable to harmless-
error analysis because the harm cannot be measured. The conviction must be
reversed automatically; no further prejudice showing is required.
```

### 2.3 Per-issue structural-error templates

#### *Sullivan v. Louisiana* — defective reasonable-doubt instruction

```
Assignment [X] presents a defective reasonable-doubt instruction. In
Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 281-82 (1993), the United States
Supreme Court held — in a Louisiana case — that a constitutionally
deficient reasonable-doubt instruction is a structural error not subject
to harmless-error review. The right to a jury verdict beyond a reasonable
doubt is a "basic protection[] [whose] precise effects are unmeasurable,
but without which a criminal trial cannot reliably serve its function."
Id. at 281. The conviction must be reversed.
```

#### *McCoy v. Louisiana* — counsel concedes guilt over objection

```
Assignment [X] presents counsel's concession of guilt over Mr. [Defendant]'s
explicit objection. In McCoy v. Louisiana, 584 U.S. 414 (2018), the United
States Supreme Court held — again, in a Louisiana case — that when a
defendant insists on maintaining innocence, defense counsel cannot concede
guilt; doing so violates the Sixth Amendment right to autonomy in defense
and is structural error requiring automatic reversal. Id. at 427-28. The
record reflects Mr. [Defendant]'s objection at [Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p.
___, ll. ___-___] and counsel's concession at [cite]. The conviction must
be reversed.
```

#### *Ramos v. Louisiana* — non-unanimous verdict

```
Assignment [X] presents a non-unanimous jury verdict. In Ramos v. Louisiana,
590 U.S. 83 (2020), the United States Supreme Court held that the Sixth
Amendment requires unanimous jury verdicts in state felony trials. The jury
verdict in this case was [10-2 / 11-1 / specify]. (Verdict Form, R. Vol.
___, p. ___; Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___, ll. ___.) Ramos applies to all
non-final cases pending on direct review when Ramos was decided (April 20,
2020). [If the verdict here was rendered AFTER Ramos, address that the
verdict directly violates Ramos. If BEFORE Ramos, address Edwards v.
Vannoy, 593 U.S. 255 (2021) and the retroactivity question.] The verdict
is structurally invalid and the conviction must be reversed.
```

> **Retroactivity note (Ramos):** *Edwards v. Vannoy*, 593 U.S. 255 (2021), held that *Ramos* does not apply retroactively to convictions that became final before *Ramos* was decided (April 20, 2020). For direct-appeal cases, *Ramos* applies to any case pending on direct review when *Ramos* was decided or thereafter. Confirm the timing carefully.

## 3. Constitutional Harmless Error — *Chapman v. California*

For federal constitutional errors that are NOT structural, *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), governs. The State must prove the error harmless **beyond a reasonable doubt**. Burden is on the State, not the defense.

### 3.1 The Chapman Standard

> "Before a federal constitutional error can be held harmless, the court must be able to declare a belief that it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt." *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967).

**Practical consequences:**
- The defendant does NOT have to prove the error caused the conviction.
- The State must affirmatively prove the contrary — that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Where the appellate court has any reasonable doubt about whether the error contributed to the verdict, the conviction must be reversed.

### 3.2 Errors subject to Chapman

- 4th Amendment evidence wrongly admitted (where suppression denial reversed)
- 5th Amendment Miranda violations / involuntary statements
- 6th Amendment Confrontation Clause violations (*Crawford v. Washington*)
- Due-process violations not amounting to structural error
- Brady violations (assessed under *Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419 (1995), which incorporates harmless-error principles)
- Most jury-instruction errors (omitted element under *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999))
- Improper admission of co-defendant statements (*Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123 (1968))
- Restriction on cross-examination (*Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986))
- Improper prosecutorial closing argument touching constitutional rights
- Right-to-counsel violations not amounting to total deprivation

### 3.3 Drafting language for Chapman analysis

**Template — D.6 Prejudice (Chapman):**

```
The error in admitting [tainted evidence / statement / testimony] is a
federal constitutional error subject to harmless-error analysis under
Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). The State bears the burden
of proving the error harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. The State
cannot meet that burden here.

[Walk through the record:]
 - [Summarize what the State's case looked like WITHOUT the tainted evidence]
 - [Identify the centrality of the tainted evidence: how the State used it
    in opening, examination, closing argument]
 - [Address the strength of the remaining evidence]
 - [Identify any inconsistencies, weak identifications, or credibility issues
    in the remaining evidence]

[Quote from State's closing argument or examination if the State emphasized
the tainted evidence:]
 (Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___, ll. ___-___): "[State's emphasis on tainted
 evidence]."

[Cite to authority establishing that similar errors were not harmless under
Chapman:]
 [Authority]

The State cannot prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the [tainted evidence]
did not contribute to the verdict. The error is not harmless. The conviction
must be reversed.
```

### 3.4 Harmless-error factors (for Chapman analysis)

Courts assess Chapman harmless error using factors including:

1. **Centrality of tainted evidence to the State's case.** Was it the only evidence on a contested element? Or was it cumulative of other admitted evidence?
2. **Strength of the untainted evidence.** Could the verdict be sustained on the untainted evidence alone?
3. **State's emphasis.** Did the prosecutor highlight the tainted evidence in opening, examination, or closing? Repeat references in closing argument suggest the State viewed it as central.
4. **Whether a curative instruction was given.** A timely, specific curative instruction may mitigate the harm; a general instruction or no instruction does not.
5. **Cumulative effect.** Where multiple errors are present, even individually-harmless errors may, in combination, require reversal under cumulative-error doctrine.
6. **Defense theory at trial.** If the defense theory hinged on disputing the tainted evidence (e.g., motion to suppress denied; defense at trial argued the statement was coerced), the tainted evidence was central by definition.

### 3.5 The omitted-element overlay (*Neder*)

For constitutional errors involving omission of an element from the jury instruction, *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 17 (1999), holds that the error is subject to Chapman harmless-error review. The question becomes whether the omission was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt — i.e., whether the record establishes the omitted element so overwhelmingly that no rational jury could have found otherwise.

> Distinguish *Sullivan v. Louisiana*: omission of a particular element is Chapman-harmless-error reviewable; defective instruction on the *standard of proof itself* (reasonable doubt) is structural under *Sullivan*.

## 4. Non-Constitutional Harmless Error — La. C.Cr.P. Art. 921

For non-constitutional errors, La. C.Cr.P. Art. 921 governs.

> "A judgment or ruling shall not be reversed by an appellate court because of any error, defect, irregularity, or variance which does not affect substantial rights of the accused."

### 4.1 The Art. 921 Standard

The error does not warrant reversal unless it "affects substantial rights of the accused." Louisiana adopted the federal harmless-error analysis from *Sullivan v. Louisiana*: an error is harmless only when "the guilty verdict actually rendered in this trial was surely unattributable to the error." *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275, 279 (1993); *State v. Johnson*, 94-1379 (La. 11/27/95), 664 So.2d 94, 102.

### 4.2 Errors subject to Art. 921

- Most evidentiary rulings (relevance, hearsay where not Confrontation Clause, expert qualification)
- 404(b) admissions (unless framed as a due-process violation)
- Procedural irregularities (continuance denials, etc.)
- Improper closing argument not constitutional in nature
- Witness sequestration violations
- Discovery rule violations

### 4.3 Drafting language for Art. 921 analysis

**Template — D.6 Prejudice (Art. 921):**

```
The error in [admitting / excluding] [evidence] is a non-constitutional
error subject to La. C.Cr.P. Art. 921. Reversal is required where the
error "affects substantial rights of the accused." Louisiana courts apply
the Sullivan framework: an error is harmless only when "the guilty verdict
actually rendered in this trial was surely unattributable to the error."
Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 279 (1993); State v. Johnson, 94-1379
(La. 11/27/95), 664 So.2d 94, 102.

[Walk through the record:]
 - [Centrality of the [evidence] to the contested issue]
 - [State's use of the [evidence] in opening, examination, closing]
 - [Strength of the State's case absent the [evidence]]
 - [Effect of the [evidence] on the jury — quote any State's closing
    argument that emphasized it]

The error affected substantial rights. It is not harmless under Art. 921.
The conviction must be reversed.
```

### 4.4 Burden allocation under Art. 921

Louisiana courts have not always articulated the burden cleanly. Some opinions place the burden on the appellant to show that the error affected substantial rights; others (especially in constitutional-adjacent cases) place a quasi-Chapman burden on the State. For brief drafting:

- Where the issue is plainly non-constitutional, frame as: "The error affected substantial rights because [reasons]."
- Where the issue has a constitutional dimension, argue Chapman as the primary framework and Art. 921 as the alternative — even if the appellate court accepts the issue as merely non-constitutional, the brief has preserved the harmless-error argument.

## 5. Per-Issue Harmless-Error Templates

Below are templated D.6 paragraphs for the most common appellate issue types, calibrated to the appropriate framework.

### 5.1 Suppression denial — improperly admitted evidence (Chapman)

```
The trial court's denial of the motion to suppress and the resulting
admission of [physical evidence / statement / identification] is a federal
constitutional error subject to Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967).
The State must prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

The [tainted evidence] was the centerpiece of the State's case on Count
[X]. (Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___, ll. ___ — State's opening argument
referencing [tainted evidence]; Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___, ll. ___ —
State's closing argument referencing [tainted evidence].) Without the
[tainted evidence], the State's case rests on [identify other evidence
and weaknesses]. The State cannot establish beyond a reasonable doubt that
the verdict would have been the same without the [tainted evidence]. The
conviction must be reversed.
```

### 5.2 Evidentiary error — La. C.E. Art. 404(B) (Art. 921 — sometimes Chapman)

```
The trial court's admission of evidence of Mr. [Defendant]'s prior [acts]
under La. C.E. Art. 404(B) was not harmless under Art. 921. The other-acts
evidence was offered ostensibly for [permitted purpose — e.g., motive,
intent], but its actual effect was to invite the jury to convict on
character — that Mr. [Defendant] is a "criminal type" and therefore
committed the charged offense. (See Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___, ll. ___
— State's closing argument.)

The contested issue at trial was [identify], not motive or intent. (Defense
opening, Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___, ll. ___.) The other-acts evidence had
no permissible bearing on the contested issue. Its admission was reasonably
likely to have influenced the verdict. La. C.Cr.P. Art. 921. The conviction
must be reversed.
```

### 5.3 Confrontation Clause / *Crawford* violation (Chapman)

```
The trial court's admission of [hearsay declarant]'s testimonial statement
without an opportunity for prior cross-examination is a Confrontation
Clause violation under Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004). The
error is constitutional and is reviewed for harmlessness beyond a reasonable
doubt. Chapman, 386 U.S. at 24.

[Hearsay declarant]'s statement was central to [the State's identification
case / the proof of the contested element]. (Trial Tr. Vol. ___, p. ___,
ll. ___.) The State emphasized the statement in closing. (Trial Tr. Vol.
___, p. ___, ll. ___.) The remaining evidence is [weak / circumstantial /
identify deficiencies]. The State cannot meet its burden under Chapman.
The conviction must be reversed.
```

### 5.4 Sufficiency of the evidence (no harmless-error analysis)

```
Sufficiency review is not subject to harmless-error analysis. If the
evidence is constitutionally insufficient under Jackson v. Virginia, 443
U.S. 307 (1979), reversal is mandatory and no retrial is permitted.
Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 (1978).
```

### 5.5 Excessive sentence (no harmless-error analysis on remand)

```
A constitutionally excessive sentence under La. Const. Art. I, § 20, is
not subject to harmless-error analysis. Where the sentence is excessive,
it must be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing. The conviction
on the underlying offense stands; only the sentence is vacated.
```

### 5.6 Errors patent — illegal sentence (no harmless-error analysis)

```
An illegal sentence is errors patent under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 920(2) and is
correctable at any time under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 882. No harmless-error
analysis applies. The illegal portion of the sentence must be vacated and
the matter remanded for resentencing within the statutory limits.
```

### 5.7 Jury instruction error (Chapman or Sullivan)

```
[If defective reasonable-doubt instruction:]
The defective reasonable-doubt instruction is structural error under
Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275 (1993). No harmless-error analysis
applies. The conviction must be reversed.

[If omitted element:]
The omission of [element] from the jury instruction is constitutional
error reviewed for harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. Neder v. United
States, 527 U.S. 1, 17 (1999). [Walk through whether the omitted element
was overwhelmingly established by the record. If not, the error is not
harmless.]
```

### 5.8 Batson violation (structural)

```
A successful Batson challenge is structural error not subject to harmless-
error analysis. Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79 (1986); Vasquez v. Hillery,
474 U.S. 254 (1986). The conviction must be reversed automatically.
```

### 5.9 Cumulative error

```
Even if individual errors are individually harmless, their cumulative
effect may require reversal. Cumulative-error analysis assesses whether
the aggregate effect of multiple errors deprived the defendant of a fair
trial. [Authority — verify Louisiana cumulative-error doctrine; federal
authority includes United States v. Bell, 367 F.3d 452 (5th Cir. 2004).]

In this case, the [enumerate errors]. Each error individually compromised
[identify aspect of trial]. In the aggregate, they produced a trial that
fell below the standard of fundamental fairness. The convictions must be
reversed.
```

Before finalizing the brief:

- [ ] Every non-structural assignment has a D.6 Prejudice / Harmless-Error subsection
- [ ] The subsection identifies whether the framework is Chapman, Art. 921, or structural
- [ ] The burden allocation is stated correctly (State for Chapman; defendant for Art. 921 — though briefs often blur this)
- [ ] The record walk-through cites specific transcript pages and lines
- [ ] State's opening / examination / closing argument is quoted where it emphasized the tainted evidence
- [ ] Cumulative error is raised where multiple errors apply
- [ ] Structural errors are identified as such with no harmless-error analysis
- [ ] Sufficiency challenges acknowledge no harmless-error analysis applies
