# Standards of Review by Issue — Louisiana Direct Appeal

The standard of review controls how the appellate court evaluates each assignment of error. Stating the wrong standard, or skipping the standard altogether, is the most common technical defect in Louisiana criminal appellate briefs. This reference provides the controlling standard for every common appellate issue.

For every assignment of error in the brief, identify the standard, name it explicitly in the D.2 Standard-of-Review subsection of the Argument, and cite the controlling authority.

## Detailed Standard-of-Review Discussion

### 1. Sufficiency of the Evidence — *Jackson v. Virginia*

**Standard:** Whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, **any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt**. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

**Louisiana application:**
- *State v. Captville*, 448 So.2d 676, 678 (La. 1984) — adopting *Jackson* as the Louisiana standard
- *State v. Mussall*, 523 So.2d 1305, 1310 (La. 1988) — Jackson methodology applies to all essential elements; appellate court does not substitute its own credibility findings for the trier of fact's
- For circumstantial evidence: La. R.S. 15:438 — every reasonable hypothesis of innocence must be excluded; in Louisiana, this is treated as a component of *Jackson* sufficiency review

**Drafting language:**

> "Sufficiency of the evidence is reviewed under *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979). Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, the question is whether any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 319. Louisiana adopted this standard in *State v. Captville*, 448 So.2d 676, 678 (La. 1984). When the case turns on circumstantial evidence, every reasonable hypothesis of innocence must be excluded. La. R.S. 15:438."

**Critical points:**
- Sufficiency review does NOT require contemporaneous objection. The issue is reviewable on appeal if assigned.
- A successful sufficiency challenge results in OUTRIGHT REVERSAL with judgment of acquittal — Double Jeopardy bars retrial. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1 (1978).
- Distinguish "sufficiency" (Jackson) from "weight of the evidence" (preserved only by motion for new trial under Art. 851(1)).

---

### 2. Constitutional Rulings — De Novo

**Standard:** Pure constitutional questions are reviewed de novo. The appellate court substitutes its judgment for the trial court's.

**Common applications:**
- 4th Amendment — was a search reasonable?
- 5th Amendment — was a statement obtained in violation of *Miranda*?
- 6th Amendment Confrontation Clause — was a testimonial statement admitted in violation of *Crawford*?
- Due Process — did pretrial identification procedures violate due process?
- Equal Protection — was a peremptory challenge race-based under *Batson*?

**Drafting language:**

> "Whether [trial court ruling] violates the [constitutional provision] is a question of law reviewed de novo. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690, 699 (1996); *State v. Hunt*, 2009-1589 (La. 12/1/09), 25 So.3d 746, 751 (legal conclusions on suppression de novo)."

**Mixed-question caveat:** Most constitutional questions in criminal appeals are mixed questions of law and fact (e.g., whether the totality of circumstances on a Miranda waiver shows voluntariness). The legal framework is reviewed de novo; subsidiary factual findings (e.g., what officer X said to defendant Y) are reviewed deferentially. State the bifurcation explicitly:

> "The legal question of [whether X violates Y constitutional provision] is reviewed de novo. The trial court's subsidiary factual findings are reviewed for clear error or abuse of discretion."

---

### 3. Evidentiary Rulings — Abuse of Discretion

**Standard:** Trial court's evidentiary rulings — relevance (Art. 401), prejudice (Art. 403), hearsay (Art. 802), other-acts evidence (Art. 404(B)), expert qualification and methodology (Art. 702 / *Daubert*), demonstrative evidence — are reviewed for **abuse of discretion**.

**Anchor:**
- *State v. Mosby*, 595 So.2d 1135, 1138-39 (La. 1992) — abuse of discretion standard for evidentiary rulings (relevancy and admissibility determinations)
- *State v. Magee*, 2011-0574 (La. 9/28/12), 103 So.3d 285 — abuse of discretion applied across multiple evidentiary rulings
- For 404(B) specifically: *State v. Taylor*, 2001-1638 (La. 1/14/03), 838 So.2d 729, 741

**Drafting language:**

> "Evidentiary rulings under La. C.E. Art. [X] are reviewed for abuse of discretion. *State v. Mosby*, 595 So.2d 1135, 1138-39 (La. 1992). The legal predicates for admission — [identify, e.g., notice for 404(B), foundation for chain of custody] — are reviewed de novo."

**Caveat:** The *legal predicate* for an evidentiary ruling (e.g., whether Crawford bars admission of testimonial hearsay; whether the State gave statutory 404(B) notice) is reviewed de novo, not for abuse of discretion. Bifurcate.

### 4. Sentencing Review — Two Standards

#### 4a. Constitutional Excessiveness — La. Const. Art. I, § 20

**Standard:** A sentence violates Louisiana Constitution Art. I, § 20 if it is "grossly disproportionate to the severity of the crime" or if it amounts to "the purposeless and needless imposition of pain and suffering." *State v. Bonanno*, 384 So.2d 355 (La. 1980). The reviewing court applies a **manifest abuse of discretion** standard.

**Anchor:**
- La. Const. Art. I, § 20 (Louisiana excessive-punishment clause — broader than 8th Amendment)
- *State v. Bonanno*, 384 So.2d 355, 358 (La. 1980)
- *State v. Cook*, 95-2784 (La. 5/31/96), 674 So.2d 957, 958 — wide trial-court discretion in sentencing within statutory limits; sentence not set aside as excessive absent manifest abuse of discretion

**Prerequisites:**
- A motion to reconsider sentence under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 881.1 must have been filed within 30 days of sentencing. *State v. Mims*, 619 So.2d 1059 (La. 1993). Without the motion, the issue is waived (unless the sentence is illegal — errors patent).

**Drafting language:**

> "A sentence is constitutionally excessive under La. Const. Art. I, § 20 if it is grossly disproportionate to the severity of the crime or amounts to the purposeless and needless imposition of pain and suffering. *State v. Bonanno*, 384 So.2d 355, 358 (La. 1980). Review is for manifest abuse of discretion. *State v. Cook*, 95-2784 (La. 5/31/96), 674 So.2d 957, 958. [DEFENDANT] preserved this issue by timely motion to reconsider sentence under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 881.1, filed [date]. *State v. Mims*, 619 So.2d 1059 (La. 1993)."

#### 4b. Mandatory-Minimum Downward Departure — *Dorthey*

**Standard:** A defendant may challenge a mandatory minimum sentence as constitutionally excessive as applied. The trial court has discretion to depart downward from a mandatory minimum where the sentence is "constitutionally excessive" — i.e., would make no measurable contribution to acceptable goals of punishment, or would be grossly out of proportion to the severity of the crime. *State v. Dorthey*, 623 So.2d 1276 (La. 1993).

The reviewing court evaluates the trial court's *Dorthey* analysis (or refusal to engage in *Dorthey* analysis) for **abuse of discretion**.

**Anchor:**
- *State v. Dorthey*, 623 So.2d 1276 (La. 1993)
- *State v. Johnson*, 97-1906 (La. 3/4/98), 709 So.2d 672, 676 — narrowing *Dorthey*; mandatory minimums presumed constitutional, departure requires clear and convincing evidence rebutting the presumption

#### 4c. Illegal Sentence — Errors Patent

**Standard:** Reviewed de novo as errors patent under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 882 and Art. 920(2). Does not require preservation by motion to reconsider sentence.

### 5. Motion to Suppress — Mixed Standard

**Standard:** Bifurcated.
- **Legal questions and mixed questions of law and fact** — de novo. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690, 699 (1996).
- **Subsidiary credibility findings** (e.g., whether the officer's testimony about defendant's consent is credible) — abuse of discretion / clearly wrong.

**Anchor:**
- *State v. Hunt*, 2009-1589 (La. 12/1/09), 25 So.3d 746, 751 — legal findings de novo; factual/credibility findings entitled to deference unless clearly contrary to the evidence
- *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690 (1996)

**Drafting language:**

> "On a motion to suppress, the trial court's legal conclusions are reviewed de novo. *State v. Hunt*, 2009-1589 (La. 12/1/09), 25 So.3d 746, 751. Subsidiary findings of fact, including credibility determinations, are entitled to deference and will not be disturbed unless clearly contrary to the evidence. *Id.*; *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690 (1996)."

---

### 6. Jury Instruction Errors

**Standard:** Whether the trial court correctly stated the law in a jury instruction is a legal question reviewed **de novo**. *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999). Refusal to give a defense-requested instruction is reviewed for the legal correctness of the proposed instruction (de novo) and, if legally correct, whether the refusal was harmful.

**Preservation:** La. C.Cr.P. Art. 801 — objection to a jury instruction must be made before the jury retires. La. C.Cr.P. Art. 802 — refusal to give a requested charge requires written submission of the proposed charge into the record.

**Harmless-error overlay:** Constitutional jury-instruction error (e.g., omission of an element, defective reasonable-doubt instruction) is subject to *Chapman v. California* harmless-error review — except *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), which holds that a constitutionally defective reasonable-doubt instruction is **structural** and not subject to harmless-error analysis.

---

### 7. Batson Challenges — Mixed Standard

**Standard:** Three-step *Batson* framework, with mixed standards at each step:
1. **Prima facie showing** of discriminatory peremptory challenge — reviewed for clear error.
2. **Race-neutral explanation** by the State — legal sufficiency reviewed de novo; credibility of explanation reviewed for clear error.
3. **Trial court's ultimate finding** of purposeful discrimination — reviewed for clear error, with great deference to the trial court's ability to assess demeanor.

**Anchor:**
- *Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986)
- *Snyder v. Louisiana*, 552 U.S. 472 (2008) — clear-error review of step 3, but no deference where the trial court made no specific finding
- *Hernandez v. New York*, 500 U.S. 352 (1991) — race-neutral explanation step

**Critical:** Because *Snyder* holds that no deference is owed where the trial court did not make a specific finding, the brief must scrutinize the trial transcript for what the trial court actually said.

---

### 8. Bench-Trial Factual Findings — Manifest Error

**Standard:** Where the case was tried to the bench and the trial court made factual findings, those findings are reviewed for **manifest error** / clearly wrong. *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840 (La. 1989) [VERIFY — Rosell is civil but criminal courts often cite it]. Two-prong test:
1. There was no reasonable factual basis for the finding; AND
2. The finding is clearly wrong.

**Note:** Manifest-error review applies only to the trial court's own factual findings, not to a jury verdict. Sufficiency review of a jury verdict is governed by *Jackson v. Virginia*.

---

### 9. Habitual-Offender Adjudication — Bifurcated

**Standard:**
- **Legal predicate questions** (e.g., whether a prior conviction qualifies under La. R.S. 15:529.1; whether the cleansing period had expired; whether a prior plea was Boykin-compliant) — de novo.
- **Factual findings** (e.g., identity of defendant as the same person convicted of the prior offense) — manifest error.

**Anchor:** *State v. Shelton*, 621 So.2d 769 (La. 1993).

---

### 10. IAC on Direct Appeal — De Novo (Where Reviewable)

Most IAC claims in Louisiana require a post-conviction evidentiary hearing and are NOT reviewable on direct appeal. *State v. Ratcliff*, 416 So.2d 528 (La. 1982). Exception: where the trial record itself establishes both Strickland prongs, IAC may be reviewed on direct appeal.

**Standard (where reviewable):** De novo on the *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), two-prong test:
1. Counsel's performance was deficient (fell below objective standards of reasonableness).
2. The deficient performance prejudiced the defense (reasonable probability of different outcome).

**Drafting note:** This skill (dw-appellate-brief-builder-crim) generally defers IAC claims to PCR per `dw-post-conviction-relief-crim`. Direct-appeal IAC briefing is the exception, not the norm.

## Standards of Review the Defense Wants vs. the State Wants

A practical note: the standard of review is a battleground in many appeals. The defense generally wants the most demanding standard for the State (de novo, structural-error). The State generally wants the most deferential standard for itself (abuse of discretion, harmless-error, manifest error). Where the standard is genuinely contested, the brief should ARGUE for the correct standard before applying it. Example:

> "The State will argue that the trial court's ruling on [issue] is reviewed for abuse of discretion. That is wrong. The question presented is whether [legal proposition], which is a pure question of law subject to de novo review. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690 (1996). To the extent any subsidiary factual finding is implicated, those findings are reviewed for clear error — but the dispositive legal question is de novo."

## Module E — Quick Standard-of-Review Categories (moved from SKILL.md)

### Quick standard-of-review categories

| Category | Standard | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Pure legal questions | De novo | Constitutional rulings, statutory interpretation |
| Mixed questions of law and fact | De novo (legal); deferential (factual/credibility) | Suppression motions, ineffective-assistance claims (post-conviction) |
| Factual findings | Manifest error / clearly wrong | Trial court fact-finding after bench trial or hearing |
| Discretionary rulings | Abuse of discretion | Evidentiary rulings, continuances, severance, sentencing within range |
| Constitutional excessiveness | Manifest abuse of discretion | Excessive sentence under La. Const. Art. I, § 20 |
| Sufficiency | Jackson / Captville | Whether evidence supports conviction |
| Structural error | Automatic reversal | *Sullivan*, *McCoy*, *Ramos*, total denial of counsel |
| Harmless error (constitutional) | State proves BRD | *Chapman v. California* |
| Harmless error (non-constitutional) | Did not affect substantial rights | La. C.Cr.P. Art. 921 |

If a standard is unclear, default to the rule that the legal question is de novo and any factual or credibility predicate is deferential — and flag with `[VERIFY STANDARD]` for attorney review.
