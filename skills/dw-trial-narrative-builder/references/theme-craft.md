# Theme Craft — Construction and Callback Principles

A trial theme is not a slogan. It is the organizing principle that explains the entire defense in human terms, in three to seven words, so a juror can carry it into deliberation and explain it to the other eleven jurors. Themes are the spine of trial narrative. They are planted in opening, watered in mid-trial, and harvested in closing. This file is the construction manual.

---

## What a Theme Is

A theme is:
- **A 3-7 word organizing principle** — short enough to repeat, memorable enough to stick
- **Human, not legal** — "wrong place, wrong time" not "actus reus without mens rea"
- **Defensible by the evidence** — every theme must be supportable by the trial record; an aspirational theme that the evidence will not bear is worse than no theme
- **Repeatable verbatim** — same words, every time. Repetition is the weapon. Variation is the leak.
- **Translatable to the other eleven jurors** — when juror #4 walks into the deliberation room, she should be able to explain the defense theory to the other jurors in fewer than 10 words

A theme is NOT:
- A summary of the defense theory (a summary is a paragraph; a theme is a phrase)
- A legal argument (legal arguments are made in closing; themes are the through-line)
- The verdict ("not guilty" is a verdict; "the police took shortcuts" is a theme)
- A slogan invented by the lawyer that the evidence does not actually support

## The Three Theme Archetypes

Most criminal defense themes fall into one of three archetypes. Pick the archetype first; refine the language second.

### Archetype 1 — Procedural ("Shortcuts and Sloppy Police Work")

The defense theory is that the police, the lab, the prosecution did not do enough — and what they did do, they did badly. The State's case is built on a foundation that won't hold under examination.

**Sample theme phrases:**
- "Shortcuts and sloppy police work"
- "What the police didn't do"
- "A rush to judgment"
- "They picked their suspect and stopped looking"
- "No DNA, no fingerprints, no proof"

**When to use:** When discovery, motion practice, and audit work (chain of custody, Brady/Giglio, mobile forensic audits, crime scene audits, DNA audits) have surfaced multiple SOP failures, missing investigative steps, or contamination issues. The auditor chain in the D&W workflow is the input pipeline for this archetype.

**Mid-trial reinforcement vectors:** Each cross-examination of a law enforcement witness that surfaces a procedural failure waters this theme. The theme should appear in the chapter headers of LE cross outlines.

**Closing harvest:** Walk the jury through every procedural failure surfaced at trial, mapped to the theme. End with: "Reasonable doubt is exactly what shortcuts and sloppy police work create."

### Archetype 2 — Identity / Mistake ("Wrong Place, Wrong Time, Wrong Man")

The defense theory is that the defendant is not the person the State should be prosecuting. Identification was flawed, the wrong person was arrested, the actual offender was someone else or no one (no crime occurred).

**Sample theme phrases:**
- "Wrong place, wrong time, wrong man"
- "A case of mistaken identity"
- "They got the wrong guy"
- "One stranger, identifying another stranger, in the dark"
- "Eyewitnesses are confidently wrong"

**When to use:** When the case turns on eyewitness identification, when the defendant has an alibi (or partial alibi), when there is reason to believe a third-party suspect should have been investigated, when the State's identification evidence is weak (no DNA, no fingerprints, suggestive lineup).

**Mid-trial reinforcement vectors:** Cross of the identifying witness on perception conditions; cross of the investigating officer on the lineup procedure; alibi witness direct examination; third-party-suspect cross-references.

**Closing harvest:** Walk the jury through the State's identification evidence, demonstrate its unreliability, contrast with the defense's alibi or third-party-suspect evidence. End with: "Wrong place, wrong time, wrong man — and that is reasonable doubt."

### Archetype 3 — Justification ("He Had No Choice")

The defense theory is that the defendant did the act, but the act was justified — self-defense, defense of others, duress, justification under La. R.S. 14:18. The defense concedes (or partially concedes) the act and argues the law of justification applies.

**Sample theme phrases:**
- "He had no choice"
- "Self-defense is the law"
- "He was protecting his family"
- "The aggressor is not in the courtroom"
- "When you fear for your life, you fight for your life"

**When to use:** Self-defense, defense of others, justification cases. Less commonly: duress (note: duress is not a defense to homicide in Louisiana per *State v. Marcantel*, 388 So.2d 372 (La. 1980)).

**Mid-trial reinforcement vectors:** Cross of the State's witnesses on the victim's aggression; defense direct of self-defense witnesses; medical / threat-history exhibits.

**Closing harvest:** Walk the jury through the State's burden to disprove self-defense beyond a reasonable doubt (La. R.S. 14:20; *State v. Patterson*). Map every piece of evidence supporting reasonable belief of imminent danger to the theme. End with: "He had no choice. The law gave him a right to defend himself. The State cannot disprove that right beyond a reasonable doubt."

## Theme Planting — The Opening

A theme is planted in opening with a **memorable line** that the jury can repeat back. The line should:
1. Be the first or last sentence of the opening (often both — bookending)
2. Use the verbatim 3-7 word phrase that will appear throughout trial and in closing
3. Be tied to a concrete image or fact, not abstract
4. Be safe under La. C.Cr.P. Art. 765 / 774 (preview, not argument)

**Example planting (Archetype 1):**
> *"On the night of March 15, the police made up their minds about Mr. Cole within ninety seconds of arriving at the scene. The evidence will show you what they didn't do after that — and what they didn't do is the reason you will have a reasonable doubt at the end of this trial. This case is about shortcuts and sloppy police work."*

The theme phrase ("shortcuts and sloppy police work") is the last six words. It is concrete (anchored in "ninety seconds"). It is a preview (the evidence "will show" what they didn't do), not argument. It promises reasonable doubt without taking on a defense burden.

---

## Theme Watering — Mid-Trial

Once a theme is planted in opening, it must be echoed throughout trial. The theme is watered every time:
1. A cross-examination chapter title is keyed to the theme (e.g., "The Shortcuts That Contaminated This Scene")
2. A witness exit allows a one-sentence theme callback during the next break or the next cross
3. An exhibit is introduced that supports the theme — the exhibit number becomes a theme marker

**The Theme Tracker (Step 5 deliverable in dw-trial-narrative-builder)** tracks every reinforcement. Each row of the tracker has a "Mid-Trial Reinforcement" column. The attorney updates this column live during trial. By the end of the State's case-in-chief, every theme should have multiple mid-trial reinforcement entries — or the theme is on track to be **Dropped** (see "Bad Themes" below).

---

## Theme Harvesting — The Closing

The closing harvests the theme. Three rules:

### Rule 1 — Same Words, Same Phrasing

The theme phrase must appear in closing in the exact same words used in opening. If opening said "shortcuts and sloppy police work," closing does not say "police corner-cutting" or "lazy detective work." Same words. Repetition is the weapon.

### Rule 2 — Bookend the Closing

The theme phrase appears at the start of the closing (callback to opening) and at the end of the closing (final image for deliberation). It may also appear at key transitions throughout.

**Example bookending:**
- **Opening of closing:** *"At the start of this trial, I told you this case was about shortcuts and sloppy police work. You have now heard the evidence. Let me show you what I meant."*
- **End of closing:** *"Shortcuts and sloppy police work. That is what created this case. That is what created your reasonable doubt. And reasonable doubt has only one verdict: not guilty."*

### Rule 3 — Map Each Theme to Specific Evidence

Don't just repeat the theme — show the jury, piece by piece of evidence, why the theme is the right one. Every theme should have a closing section listing the trial evidence that watered it. The Theme Tracker provides the evidence map.

## Theme Arc — Seed, Water, Harvest

The full lifecycle:

```
OPENING (planting)        →  MID-TRIAL (watering)        →  CLOSING (harvest)
[memorable line]              [cross-exam chapters,         [bookend recitation,
[3-5 themes registered]        witness exits,                evidence-mapped
                               exhibit introductions]        theme proof,
                                                            final image]
```

The Theme Tracker is the live document tracking this arc. By the time of closing, the Theme Tracker should show, for each theme:
- WHERE it was registered (opening section)
- WHEN it was reinforced (mid-trial events with timestamps)
- HOW it will be called back (closing section)

## Bad Themes — How to Recognize and Avoid

### Too Abstract

> "This case is about justice."

What is the jury supposed to do with this? Every party in every trial wants justice. The theme tells the jury nothing concrete. **Bad theme — strike.**

### Too Literal

> "The State has not proven every element of theft of property valued at over $1,000 under La. R.S. 14:67(B)(2) beyond a reasonable doubt."

Accurate, but no juror is going to carry that into the deliberation room. **Bad theme — replace with: "This isn't a theft case. It's a misunderstanding case."**

### Too Many

> Themes 1-12 registered in opening.

Three to five themes is the cap. More than five and the jury cannot carry them. The deliberating juror needs to be able to explain the defense in a handful of organizing principles, not twelve. **Cap at 5; default to 3-4.**

### Indefensible by the Evidence

> Opening: "The evidence will show Mr. Cole was at his mother's house on March 15."
> Trial: alibi witness recants; mother testifies she does not remember.

If a theme cannot be defended by the evidence that actually comes in, the theme is **Dropped**. A dropped theme cannot be harvested in closing — referencing it in closing only reminds the jury that the defense promised something it could not deliver. The State will pounce in rebuttal. The Theme Tracker's "Status" column flags dropped themes so they are not harvested.

### Conflicting Themes

> Theme 1: "Wrong man — Mr. Cole wasn't there."
> Theme 2: "Self-defense — Mr. Cole had no choice."

These themes are mutually exclusive (if he wasn't there, he didn't defend himself; if he defended himself, he was there). Mixed-defense cases happen, but the themes must be sequenced — not concurrent. Typically the primary theme dominates the opening and closing, and the alternative theme is presented as a backup ("even if the jury believes Mr. Cole was there, the evidence shows..."). **Conflicting themes confuse juries; sequence them or drop one.**

## Callback Mechanics — The Mechanics of Repetition

The defense theme is repeated:

1. **At opening** — first or last sentence, often both (bookend)
2. **At each witness handoff** — when a defense cross of a State witness ends, the last question or the immediate transition references the theme (e.g., "So the body-cam doesn't show what the report says it shows — and that's another shortcut.")
3. **At each defense direct** — defense witnesses' testimony is structured to support the theme
4. **At each exhibit introduction** — the exhibit short name and the theme are linked
5. **At closing opening** — callback to the opening's planting line ("At the start of this trial, I told you...")
6. **At closing transitions** — each section of the closing returns to the theme
7. **At closing ending** — final image, final word

Seven contact points minimum. The Theme Tracker should reflect these contact points; if a theme appears fewer than four times across the trial, it is under-watered and the closing harvest will land weak.

## Examples From Notable Defense Closings (Illustrative)

These are illustrative templates, not specific case citations:

**Procedural archetype:**
> *"They picked their suspect, and then they stopped looking. They had a man in handcuffs by 10:00 PM, and after that, every piece of evidence in this trial was filtered through one question: how do we prove the man in handcuffs did it? That isn't investigation. That is confirmation. And confirmation produces reasonable doubt."*

**Identity archetype:**
> *"Ms. Williams was confident. She was clear. She was certain. And she was wrong. The lighting was poor. The encounter was three seconds. She had never seen Mr. Cole before in her life. Eyewitnesses are confidently wrong every day in this country, and the law tells you that confidence is not accuracy. Wrong place, wrong time, wrong man — and that is your reasonable doubt."*

**Justification archetype:**
> *"Mr. Cole did not start this fight. Mr. Cole did not want this fight. But when three men cornered him in that alley, the law gave him a right. The law gave him a right to defend himself. And the State has not — and cannot — disprove that right beyond a reasonable doubt. He had no choice. And that is your verdict: not guilty."*

## The Theme Tracker — Operating Manual

The Theme Tracker (.xlsx) is the live document tracking the theme arc. See `dw-trial-narrative-builder` STEP 5 for the column structure. Operating rules:

- **Register all themes during opening build (MODE A).** Each theme gets a row.
- **Update Mid-Trial column LIVE during trial.** This is an attorney task during trial; the skill provides the template.
- **At closing build (MODE B), populate the Closing Callback column.** Map each theme to the closing section that harvests it.
- **Update Status column.** Mark dropped themes BEFORE closing build — dropped themes do not get callbacks.
- **Cross-check Theme Tracker against the closing draft** before finalizing closing. Every theme with Status = Reinforced should have a Closing Callback entry. Any reinforced theme without a callback is a missed opportunity — flag and address.

---

*Theme craft is a defense lawyer's art, not a science. These principles are guidelines, not rules. The best themes come from the case file, not from a manual. But every theme — regardless of source — must pass the verbatim-repetition test, the evidence-supportability test, and the deliberating-juror-can-explain-it test before it earns a row in the Theme Tracker.*
