# Reid Technique and Interrogation Coercion Tactics Reference

## Reid Technique — Nine-Step Identification

The Reid Technique is the most widely used interrogation method in American law enforcement. While not per se unconstitutional, its techniques are the primary drivers of false confessions. Identify each step employed:

| Reid Step | Description | Markers in Transcript/Recording | Defense Significance |
|-----------|------------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| **Step 1: Direct Positive Confrontation** | Interrogator states certainty of guilt: "We know you did this" | "The evidence shows..."; "We already know..."; "There's no doubt..." | Creates psychological pressure; implies investigation is closed; suggests resistance is futile |
| **Step 2: Theme Development** | Interrogator offers moral justifications or excuses for the crime | "Anyone in your situation would have..."; "She provoked you..."; "It was an accident, wasn't it?" | Provides face-saving narrative; may constitute implied promise that "understandable" version will receive leniency |
| **Step 3: Handling Denials** | Interrogator cuts off or discourages denials | Interrupting defendant mid-denial; "Stop lying"; "We're past that" | Prevents defendant from maintaining innocence; creates learned helplessness |
| **Step 4: Overcoming Objections** | Interrogator addresses reasons defendant gives for innocence | "You say you weren't there, but your phone was"; redirecting rather than exploring alibi | Legitimate objections by innocent suspects are dismissed as "guilty behavior" |
| **Step 5: Maintaining Attention** | Interrogator prevents defendant from withdrawing | Moving closer; maintaining eye contact; touching defendant's arm; calling by first name | Physical proximity and touch increase psychological pressure |
| **Step 6: Handling Passive Mood** | Interrogator addresses defendant's resignation, crying, or emotional signs | "I can see this is hard for you"; "You want to get this off your chest" | Interprets emotional distress as guilt rather than as fear, confusion, or despair |
| **Step 7: Presenting the Alternative Question** | Interrogator offers two explanations — both assuming guilt | "Did you plan this or was it spur of the moment?"; "Was it because of the drugs or because she made you angry?" | THE KEY FALSE CONFESSION DRIVER — the "acceptable" alternative is still a confession; defendant who chooses the "lesser" option has confessed |
| **Step 8: Developing the Admission** | Interrogator has defendant detail the crime after initial admission | "Walk me through what happened"; "Tell me everything from the beginning" | Opportunity for contamination — interrogator may feed details the defendant did not independently know |
| **Step 9: Written/Recorded Confession** | Interrogator documents the confession | Written statement; recorded statement; "Now tell me again for the camera" | Often occurs after extensive unrecorded interrogation; the "clean" recorded version may not reflect the process that produced it |
## Additional Interrogation Techniques — Coercion Level Analysis

| Technique | Description | Markers | Coercion Level |
|-----------|------------|---------|---------------|
| **Minimization** | Downplaying the seriousness of the crime or the consequences of confessing | "This isn't as bad as you think"; "You'll feel better if you tell the truth"; "The judge will understand" | MODERATE to HIGH — research shows minimization functions as an implied promise of leniency (*Kassin & McNall*, 1991) |
| **Maximization** | Exaggerating the seriousness of the situation, the strength of the evidence, or the potential consequences | "You're looking at life"; "The evidence is overwhelming"; "Your buddy already told us everything" | MODERATE to HIGH — creates fear and despair; may constitute threat |
| **False Evidence Ploy** | Presenting fabricated evidence to the defendant | "Your DNA was on the weapon" (when it wasn't); "We have you on camera" (when there is no camera); "Your fingerprints were at the scene" (when they weren't) | HIGH — legal in most jurisdictions (*Frazier v. Cupp*, 394 U.S. 731 (1969)), but a significant factor in the voluntariness analysis and a primary driver of false confessions; particularly dangerous with vulnerable suspects |
| **Implied Promise of Leniency** | Suggesting (without explicitly promising) that cooperating will result in better outcomes | "Help yourself here"; "The DA looks at cooperation"; "Things will go a lot easier if you're honest" | MODERATE to HIGH — while explicit promises render a confession involuntary, implied promises are analyzed under totality of circumstances |
| **Good Cop / Bad Cop** | Two interrogators play contrasting roles — one aggressive, one sympathetic | One officer threatens or berates; the other offers comfort, sympathy, and a "way out" | MODERATE — the sympathetic interrogator's offers of help function as implied promises |
| **Bluff About Co-Defendant** | Telling the defendant that a co-defendant has confessed and implicated them | "Your partner already told us everything. He's putting it all on you" | HIGH — creates urgency to provide a competing narrative; may be completely fabricated |
| **Appealing to Religion / Morality** | Using the defendant's religious beliefs or moral values to encourage confession | "God knows what happened"; "You need to cleanse your conscience"; "What would your mother think?" | MODERATE — exploits emotional vulnerability |
| **Threats to Family / Third Parties** | Suggesting negative consequences for family members if defendant does not confess | "Your wife might be charged too"; "CPS is going to take your kids if you don't cooperate" | CRITICAL — threats to third parties, especially children, are among the most coercive techniques and weigh heavily toward involuntariness |
| **Futility / Inevitability** | Convincing the defendant that conviction is inevitable regardless of whether they confess | "You're going down either way. The only question is how hard"; "With or without your statement, the jury will convict you" | HIGH — creates despair and learned helplessness; undermines rational decision-making |
| **Reciprocity / Rapport** | Building personal connection, offering food/drink/comfort, then leveraging the relationship | "I went out of my way to get you this food. Now help me out"; extended rapport-building before pivoting to confrontation | MODERATE — rapport itself is legitimate, but leveraging it creates obligation pressure |
| **Strategic Use of Evidence (SUE)** | Withholding known evidence and asking open-ended questions to detect inconsistencies, then confronting with withheld evidence | Asking "Were you anywhere near the store?" knowing GPS places defendant there, then revealing GPS data after denial | LOW to MODERATE — this technique (common in PEACE model) is less coercive than Reid but is used to lock the defendant into lies before confrontation |
