# Miranda Standards — Quick Reference

This file contains the substantive Miranda framework that Module A of the confession/interrogation auditor applies. The SKILL.md provides audit *questions*; this file provides the *legal standards* the answers are measured against.

**Use this file when:** auditing a Mirandized custodial interrogation; drafting findings for a suppression motion under *Miranda v. Arizona* and La. C.Cr.P. Art. 703; preparing cross-examination of the interrogating officer on advisement and waiver compliance.

---

## 1. The Four Miranda Warnings

Per *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 444 (1966), a person in custody must be warned, prior to interrogation, of all four:

| # | Warning | Required Substance |
|---|---------|-------------------|
| 1 | Right to remain silent | "You have the right to remain silent" — must convey that the suspect can refuse to speak |
| 2 | Use of statements | "Anything you say can and will be used against you in a court of law" — must convey that statements have evidentiary weight |
| 3 | Right to counsel | "You have the right to an attorney" — must convey both right to consult before questioning and to have counsel present during questioning |
| 4 | Appointed counsel | "If you cannot afford an attorney, one will be appointed for you" — must convey indigent appointment |

**Substance over form.** *Florida v. Powell*, 559 U.S. 50, 60 (2010) — the warnings need not be a verbatim recitation, but must "reasonably convey" each right. Vagueness or omission of any element is a defect.

**Specific defect patterns:**
- Warning #3 omits "during questioning" → defective if it suggests the right is only pre-questioning
- Warning #4 conditions appointment on inability to afford "later" → defective; *Powell* found this passable but defense should challenge
- Bundled warnings ("you have rights including a lawyer") → defective if it does not separately convey each warning

## 2. Custody Determination

Miranda applies only when the suspect is in **custody**. *Stansbury v. California*, 511 U.S. 318 (1994).

### The custody test

A person is in custody when, under the totality of the circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to terminate the encounter and leave. *Yarborough v. Alvarado*, 541 U.S. 652, 663 (2004).

### Factors

| Factor | Toward Custody | Toward Non-Custody |
|--------|---------------|--------------------|
| Location | Police station, locked room, patrol car | Home, public space, suspect's vehicle |
| Restraint | Handcuffed, told not to leave, weapons drawn | Free movement, told free to leave |
| Number of officers | Multiple officers, surrounding | Single officer |
| Duration | Extended | Brief |
| Officer demeanor | Aggressive, accusatory | Conversational |
| Statements about freedom | "You can't leave" | "You're free to go" |
| Use of force or weapons | Visible | Not displayed |
| Frisk or search | Conducted | Not conducted |
| Time of day | Late night, early morning | Daytime |

### Special custody scenarios

- **Traffic stops:** Generally non-custodial. *Berkemer v. McCarty*, 468 U.S. 420 (1984). Custody can develop if stop becomes prolonged or coercive.
- **Stationhouse interviews:** Not automatically custodial. *Oregon v. Mathiason*, 429 U.S. 492 (1977). Look at totality.
- **Hospital interviews:** Fact-dependent; pain medication, mobility limits, presence of officers all relevant.
- **Juveniles:** Apply *J.D.B. v. North Carolina*, 564 U.S. 261 (2011) — the suspect's age is a relevant factor. Louisiana adds La. Ch.C. Art. 808 (juvenile custodial interrogation requires parent/attorney consultation).

## 3. Interrogation Determination

Miranda applies only to **interrogation**. *Rhode Island v. Innis*, 446 U.S. 291, 301 (1980).

Interrogation = express questioning OR its functional equivalent (any words or actions reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response).

| Action | Interrogation? |
|--------|---------------|
| Direct question | Yes |
| Confronting suspect with evidence | Yes |
| "Christian burial" speech / appeals to conscience | Yes |
| Booking questions (name, address) | Generally no — *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990) |
| Spontaneous statement (not in response to interrogation) | No |
| Conversation between officers within suspect's hearing | Yes if reasonably likely to elicit response |
| Statements to undercover agent in cell | No — *Illinois v. Perkins*, 496 U.S. 292 (1990) |

## 4. Timing Analysis Matrix

| Phase | Question | Why It Matters |
|-------|----------|---------------|
| **Pre-custody questioning** | Were any statements obtained before custody attached? | Pre-custody statements are not Miranda-protected but bear on voluntariness |
| **Moment of custody** | When did custody attach? | Defines when Miranda warnings must be given |
| **Time between custody and warnings** | How long did the suspect remain in custody before warnings? | Delay can undermine the effectiveness of warnings; cumulative pressure analysis |
| **Time between warnings and waiver** | Did the suspect have time to comprehend? | Brief, immediate waiver after warnings raises knowing/intelligent concerns |
| **Time between waiver and questioning** | Did the State begin questioning immediately? | Generally fine, but combined with other factors can show coercion |
| **Question-first / two-step interrogations** | Were unwarned statements obtained, then warnings administered, then the same statements re-elicited? | Per *Missouri v. Seibert*, 542 U.S. 600 (2004), this technique is presumptively unconstitutional |

### Seibert "question-first" red flags

1. Officer obtained unwarned statement
2. Same officer (or partnered) administered Miranda
3. Warnings did not include any cure (no break in time, no shift in interrogator, no statement that prior unwarned statement is inadmissible)
4. Same subject matter resumed
5. Unwarned and warned statements are substantively identical

If multiple flags present, the warned statement is presumptively inadmissible under *Seibert*'s plurality and Justice Kennedy's concurrence.

## 5. Waiver Validity Framework

A valid Miranda waiver must be (1) **voluntary**, (2) **knowing**, and (3) **intelligent**. *Moran v. Burbine*, 475 U.S. 412, 421 (1986).

### Voluntary

Free of police coercion. The State must show by a preponderance of the evidence that the waiver was the product of free choice. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986). In Louisiana, voluntariness of the *confession itself* (downstream of waiver) is on the State **beyond a reasonable doubt** under La. R.S. 15:452.

### Knowing

The suspect understood the rights being waived. Defects:
- Language barrier without adequate interpreter
- Mental illness or intellectual disability impairing comprehension
- Intoxication impairing comprehension
- Juvenile age + complexity of warnings
- Severe sleep deprivation

### Intelligent

The suspect understood the consequences of waiver. Distinct from "knowing" — even a person who understands the words may not grasp the strategic significance.

### Express vs. implied waiver

- **Express:** Suspect verbally or in writing waives ("I'll talk to you," signed waiver form). Strongest position for the State.
- **Implied:** Suspect makes statements after warnings without expressly waiving. *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 (2010) — silence followed by statement may be deemed a waiver. Louisiana courts apply this but defense should always challenge implied-waiver findings.

## 6. Invocation of Rights

### Right to silence

Must be **unambiguous**. *Berghuis v. Thompkins*. Equivocal statements ("I think I want to be quiet") are insufficient and police may continue questioning.

If unambiguously invoked:
- Police must "scrupulously honor" the invocation. *Michigan v. Mosley*, 423 U.S. 96 (1975).
- Re-interrogation possible only after a significant break, fresh warnings, and on a different subject matter.

### Right to counsel

Must be **unambiguous**. *Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 452 (1994). "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" is insufficient.

If unambiguously invoked under *Edwards v. Arizona*, 451 U.S. 477 (1981):
- All interrogation must cease.
- Suspect cannot be re-interrogated unless counsel is present OR suspect initiates further communication.
- The bar continues even on unrelated charges. *Arizona v. Roberson*, 486 U.S. 675 (1988).
- Once 14 days have passed since release from custody, the *Edwards* presumption ends. *Maryland v. Shatzer*, 559 U.S. 98 (2010).

## 7. Miranda Red Flag Matrix

| Red Flag | Severity | Authority |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Warnings not given prior to interrogation | CRITICAL | *Miranda v. Arizona* |
| Warnings missing one of four elements | CRITICAL | *Florida v. Powell* |
| Warnings given in language suspect doesn't speak fluently | CRITICAL | totality + *Connelly* |
| Question-first / two-step interrogation | CRITICAL | *Missouri v. Seibert* |
| Re-questioning after invocation of counsel without counsel present | CRITICAL | *Edwards v. Arizona* |
| Re-questioning after invocation of silence within minutes / same subject | SIGNIFICANT | *Michigan v. Mosley* |
| Implied waiver only, no express acknowledgment | MODERATE | *Berghuis v. Thompkins* (challengeable) |
| Juvenile interrogation without parent/attorney consultation | CRITICAL (LA) | La. Ch.C. Art. 808 |
| Suspect mentally ill / intellectually disabled / heavily intoxicated | SIGNIFICANT | knowing/intelligent prong |
| Warnings administered late (delay between custody and warning) | MODERATE | totality |
| Officer downplays or qualifies the warnings ("just a formality") | SIGNIFICANT | knowing/intelligent prong |
| No written waiver form, no recording of waiver | MODERATE | proof problem for State |
| Suspect's invocation logged but ignored | CRITICAL | totality + invocation case law |

## 8. Louisiana-Specific Considerations

- **La. R.S. 15:451** — pre-introduction showing that the confession was free and voluntary
- **La. R.S. 15:452** — State's burden of proof at suppression: **beyond a reasonable doubt** (higher than federal preponderance)
- **La. C.Cr.P. Art. 703** — motion to suppress confession; mandatory pretrial unless cause shown
- **La. Ch.C. Art. 808** — juveniles: parental and counsel notification rights before custodial interrogation
- ***State v. Blank*, 293 So.3d 1136 (La. 2020)** — totality of circumstances applied with careful scrutiny of duration, mental and physical condition, charges, and police conduct
- ***State v. Simmons*, 443 So.2d 512 (La. 1983)** — affirms beyond-reasonable-doubt voluntariness burden
