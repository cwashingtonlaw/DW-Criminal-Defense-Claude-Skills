# False Confession Research Authorities Reference

## Quick Reference — False Confession Research Authorities

| Authority | Key Finding | Application |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Kassin & Kiechel (1996) | 69% of innocent subjects signed false confessions in the ALT-key experiment; rate increased with false evidence and heightened vulnerability | Foundation for false evidence ploy challenges |
| Kassin & McNall (1991) | Minimization functions as an implied promise of leniency; maximization functions as a threat | Foundation for minimization/maximization challenges |
| Drizin & Leo (2004) | Analysis of 125 proven false confessions: mean interrogation length 16.3 hours; 33% of false confessors were juveniles; 22% were mentally disabled | Foundation for duration, juvenile, and intellectual disability challenges |
| Gudjonsson (2003) | *The Psychology of Interrogations and Confessions* — comprehensive analysis of suggestibility, compliance, and false confession mechanisms | Expert testimony foundation |
| Perillo & Kassin (2011) | False evidence ploys combined with minimization produce highest false confession rates | Foundation for combined technique challenges |
| Frenda, Berkowitz, Loftus & Fenn (2016) | Sleep deprivation significantly increases false confession risk | Foundation for sleep deprivation challenges |
| Grisso (1981, 2003) | Juveniles under 15 have significantly impaired comprehension of Miranda rights | Foundation for juvenile Miranda challenges |
| Leo & Ofshe (1998) | Taxonomy of false confessions: voluntary, compliant, persuaded | Framework for false confession type classification |
| Innocence Project data | Approximately 29% of DNA exoneration cases involved false confessions | General false confession prevalence |
