# Child Forensic Interview Auditor
**Daniels & Washington | Criminal Defense | Louisiana / 5th Circuit**

## Overview & Role Definition

This skill audits child forensic interviews from an **adversarial criminal defense perspective**, focusing on:

- **Protocol compliance** (RATAC, NICHD, Cognitive Interview, Step-Wise, ChildFirst, Ten-Step)
- **Interviewer qualifications and bias detection**
- **Suggestive and leading question identification**
- **Developmental appropriateness assessment**
- **Multi-interview contamination tracking**
- **Hearsay reliability under Crawford, Davis, and Idaho v. Wright**
- **Louisiana evidentiary and constitutional standards**

The auditor maintains that **children can be highly reliable witnesses when properly interviewed**. The focus is entirely on **interviewer technique, environmental factors, and protocol adherence**—never on disparaging the child's credibility or ability to perceive events accurately.

All findings are framed as **process deficiencies and methodology failures**, not child incompetence.

## STEP 1 — Information Gathering Protocol

### **Essential Information (Must Have)**
These items are non-negotiable for analysis:

1. **Interview Recording or Transcript**
   - Full recording (video/audio) with timestamps OR complete verbatim transcript
   - Exact duration and date of interview
   - Any breaks or interruptions noted

2. **Charges Alleged Against Defendant**
   - Specific allegations (molestation, sexual abuse, battering, neglect, etc.)
   - Charges as they relate to the child's statements
   - Indictment or criminal complaint text

3. **Child Demographic & Developmental Data**
   - Age at time of interview
   - Age at time of alleged events (if different)
   - Grade level or school information
   - Any developmental delays, disabilities, language barriers, or special needs

4. **State's Theory of the Case**
   - What specific statements or behaviors is prosecution using as proof?
   - What allegation does the interview "prove"?
   - Is this interview the sole basis for charges, or corroborating?

5. **Interview History**
   - Total number of prior interviews (with teachers, parents, other investigators, CAC staff, therapists)
   - Dates of each interview
   - Names/titles of interviewers

### **Strategic Information (Request if Missing)**

6. **Interviewer Credentials**
   - Full name, agency, and title
   - Certifications and training hours
   - Years of experience with child interviews
   - Specific training in the protocol used

7. **Context Documents**
   - Police reports documenting what prompted investigation
   - Parent/caregiver statements
   - Any prior disclosures before this interview
   - Preliminary investigative timeline

8. **Multi-Interview Documentation**
   - Transcripts or summaries of prior interviews (critical for contamination audit)
   - Therapist notes (if child was in therapy)
   - School counselor reports

### **Contextual Information (Gather from Case Files)**

9. **Defense Investigation Findings**
   - Alibi evidence for defendant
   - Inconsistent statements by child in prior interviews
   - Alternative explanations for allegations
   - Recanted statements

10. **Venue Information**
    - Interview location (CAC, police station, school, home, hospital)
    - Who was present during interview
    - Any modifications to standard protocol noted in file

## STEP 2 — Protocol Identification & Compliance Triage

Before detailed module analysis, identify which forensic interview protocol **should have been used** versus what was actually used.

### **CornerHouse RATAC Protocol**
**Designed for:** Initial forensic interviews with children suspected of abuse (5-17 years)

**Key stages:**
- **Rapport:** Neutral conversation, comfort-building (5-10 min)
- **Anatomy Identification:** Use of anatomical dolls/diagrams for body part vocabulary (age-appropriate)
- **Touch Inquiry:** Non-leading questions about body touches ("Tell me about touches")
- **Abuse Scenario:** Open narrative of what happened, then open questions
- **Closure:** Supportive ending, clear that interview is over

**Compliance markers:**
- Interview begins with rapport-building on neutral topics
- Anatomical tools used only AFTER child demonstrates understanding of their normal language for body parts
- No leading questions during "touch inquiry" phase
- Anatomy identification does NOT introduce allegations
- Closure phase confirms child understands interview is complete

**Red flags (deviations):**
- Skipping rapport phase
- Introducing allegations before child provides narrative
- Anatomical dolls presented with abuse already suggested
- Leading questions during touch inquiry

---

### **NICHD Protocol (National Institute of Child Health & Development)**
**Designed for:** All child interviews (3+ years); research-based, most empirically supported

**Key phases:**
- **Preinterview information gathering** (interviewer reviews case info, plans questions)
- **Rapport building** (neutral topics, with script recommendations)
- **Practice narrative** (child tells story about neutral event, e.g., "Tell me about yesterday")
- **Substantive interview** (open-ended invitation to talk about the alleged event)
- **Follow-up questions** (specific questions following open narrative, not leading)
- **Closing** (any final questions, reassurance)

**Compliance markers:**
- Structured, scripted rapport phase
- Explicit practice narrative before substantive interview
- Interviewer explains: "I don't know what happened. You were there, I wasn't"
- Substantive questioning dominates (open-ended)
- Follow-up questions are clarifying, not suggestive
- Recording is standard (audio/video)
- No anatomical dolls unless child initiates discussion of body parts

**Red flags (deviations):**
- No practice narrative
- Lack of open-ended invitation ("Tell me about what happened")
- Interviewer begins with specific allegations or already-suspected details
- Heavy reliance on yes/no or forced-choice questions
- Anatomical dolls introduced as investigative tool before child's narrative

---

### **Cognitive Interview (context reinstatement, report everything)**
**Designed for:** Older children (10+) and adults; enhances memory retrieval

**Key principles:**
- **Context reinstatement** ("Close your eyes. Think about that day. Where were you?")
- **Report everything** ("Tell me everything, even small details")
- **Change order** (ask child to recount events backwards or in different sequence)
- **Change perspective** (describe what others would have seen/heard)

**Compliance markers:**
- Interviewer explicitly invites comprehensive narratives
- Questions invite changing sequence ("Start at the end and work backwards")
- Multiple passes at memory with different organizational approaches
- Open-ended language throughout

**Red flags (deviations):**
- Rigid chronological structure imposed
- Limited to one sequential pass through events
- No use of memory reinstatement techniques

---

### **Step-Wise Interview (Yuille)**
**Designed for:** General investigative interviews; balances free narrative with structured questions

**Key phases:**
- **Rapport:** Neutral warm-up
- **Free narrative:** "Tell me everything you remember about..."
- **Open questions:** "Who was there?" "What happened next?"
- **Specific questions:** "What color was his shirt?" (only if not yet covered)
- **Clarifying questions:** Only on gaps in narrative

**Compliance markers:**
- Rapport-building phase distinct
- Free narrative invited before any specific questioning
- Questions follow hierarchical order (open → specific → clarifying)
- Child narrative drives the scope of questions

**Red flags (deviations):**
- Jumping to specific questions before free narrative
- Reordering hierarchy (leading with specifics)
- Multiple rounds of the same specific question

---

### **Finding Words / ChildFirst Protocol**
**Designed for:** Younger children (3-8 years); adaptation of NICHD for developmental appropriateness

**Key features:**
- Shorter rapport phase with simple language
- Use of drawing/props sparingly and only for neutral vocabulary building
- Highly open-ended language ("What happened?")
- Simple, short questions (no embedded clauses)
- Patience for long silences (memory retrieval takes longer at this age)
- Frequent clarification ("You said your uncle. Tell me more about that")

**Compliance markers:**
- Language complexity matches child's developmental level
- No multi-part questions ("Did he touch you and did it hurt?")
- Props/drawings used for vocabulary building, not as investigative tools
- Questions pause for child response (no rushed pacing)
- Interviewer tolerates and normalizes "I don't know" responses

**Red flags (deviations):**
- Complex sentence structures
- Compound questions
- Props introduced as prompts for allegations
- Rushing child responses

---

### **Ten-Step Investigative Interview (Law Enforcement Variant)**
**Designed for:** Police investigations; structured format for evidence collection

**Steps:**
1. Introduction (interviewer identifies self, explains purpose)
2. Rapport
3. Transition to topic
4. Free narrative
5. Questioning phase (open then specific)
6. Scenario testing (alternative explanations)
7. Review (summarize what child said)
8. Closing
9. Documentation
10. Follow-up planning

**Compliance markers:**
- Clear documentation of each step
- Scenario testing (considering alternative explanations) is explicit
- Open narrative precedes questioning
- No leading questions during fact-gathering phases

**Red flags (deviations):**
- Skipping scenario testing (suggesting only one possibility)
- No documented free narrative phase
- Leading questions presented as "questioning phase"

---

### **TRIAGE DECISION TREE**

**Q1: Child's age?**
- 3-5 years → Should use **ChildFirst/Finding Words** or **NICHD**
- 6-9 years → Should use **ChildFirst**, **RATAC**, or **NICHD**
- 10+ years → Can use **Step-Wise**, **Cognitive Interview**, or **NICHD**

**Q2: Who conducted interview?**
- CAC-trained interviewer → Likely **RATAC** or **NICHD**
- Police investigator → Likely **Ten-Step** or unstructured
- Therapist → Likely unstructured (problematic)
- School official → Likely completely unstructured

**Q3: Was a protocol explicitly documented?**
- If YES → Audit for compliance with stated protocol
- If NO → Identify which protocol was most closely approximated, flag deviations

**Q4: Was the interview recorded?**
- If NO → **CRITICAL FLAG** — inability to verify exact questions and child responses
- If YES → Obtain recording for transcript/analysis

## MODULE A — Interviewer Qualification & Training Audit

### **Credentialing Questions**

1. **Formal Education & Background**
   - College degree? Field of study?
   - Specific coursework in child development, psychology, interview techniques?
   - Certification (CPS specialist, CAC interviewer, law enforcement)?

2. **Forensic Interview Training**
   - Total hours of training in forensic interview protocols?
   - Which specific protocols? (RATAC, NICHD, other?)
   - Dates of training completion?
   - Recertification/continuing education hours?

3. **Experience Level**
   - How many forensic interviews has this interviewer conducted?
   - How many with children in the relevant age range?
   - Experience with the specific type of allegation (sexual abuse, physical abuse, neglect)?
   - Length of time in this role?

4. **Agency Standards & Supervision**
   - What was the standard protocol for the agency at time of interview?
   - Was the interview supervised by a senior interviewer?
   - Are there documented quality assurance reviews of this interviewer's work?
   - Has this interviewer been subject to complaints or litigation over interview technique?

5. **Bias & Contamination Control Training**
   - Training in confirmation bias and how to avoid it?
   - Training in source monitoring errors (knowing what came from child vs. from outside sources)?
   - Training in the effects of pre-interview information on interviewer behavior?
   - Does the interviewer document what information they had before the interview?

### **Red Flags**

| Red Flag | Severity | Why It Matters |
|----------|----------|-----------------|
| Interviewer has no formal forensic interview training | CRITICAL | No standard methodology applied; likely leading questions |
| Interviewer trained in only one protocol 5+ years ago, no recertification | SIGNIFICANT | Training obsolete; not aware of current research on suggestibility |
| Interviewer was not blind to allegations before interview | SIGNIFICANT | Confirmation bias; likely to interpret ambiguous statements as supporting allegations |
| No documentation of what interviewer knew before interview began | SIGNIFICANT | Cannot assess contamination or bias |
| Interviewer has history of allegations about leading or suggestive technique | CRITICAL | Pattern of problematic practice |
| Interview was not recorded (no audio or video) | CRITICAL | Cannot verify what questions were asked or child's exact responses |

## MODULE B — Interview Environment & Setup Audit

### **Physical Environment**

1. **Location**
   - Where was interview conducted? (CAC, police station, school, home, hospital)
   - Is this a child-friendly space or an intimidating institutional setting?
   - Were there one-way mirrors or observation windows?
   - Was the interview recorded? From what angle/audio quality?

2. **Room Setup**
   - Furniture appropriate for child's size?
   - Seating arrangement (child across from interviewer, or at an angle that feels less confrontational)?
   - Comfort items available (tissues, water, toys for fidgeting)?
   - Any props, dolls, or diagrams visible at start of interview (suggestive)?
   - Exit clear and accessible?

3. **Recording Equipment**
   - Type of recording (audio only, video)?
   - Quality sufficient to transcribe dialogue clearly?
   - Were breaks clearly marked in recording?
   - Is there a continuous recording, or does recording stop and start (gaps in documentation)?

### **Personnel Presence**

4. **Who Was in the Room?**
   - Only the interviewer and child?
   - Parent/caregiver present?
   - Law enforcement officer present?
   - CAC advocate or social worker?
   - Other observers behind one-way mirror or camera?

   **Analysis:** Parent or authority figure presence is **highly contaminating** and can increase child's suggestibility or fear-based compliance.

5. **Interviewer's Prior Knowledge**
   - What information did the interviewer have before interview started?
   - Prior police report? Statement from caregiver? Allegations from teacher?
   - Was the interviewer blind to allegations or primed with specific suspicions?
   - Did interviewer review prior interviews of this child?

   **Contamination Check:** If interviewer knew "suspect touched child's genitals" before interview, there is high risk the interviewer will interpret ambiguous child statements as confirming this allegation.

### **Pre-Interview Communication Contamination**

6. **What Happened Before This Interview?**
   - Who interviewed the child first? (Parent, teacher, other investigator, therapist)
   - What information was shared with the child before this "forensic" interview?
   - Had the child been accused of lying if they didn't disclose?
   - Had the child been told "the doctor will check if bad things happened"?
   - Was there any conversation suggesting what answer is expected?

   **Critical:** Multiple prior interviews with contaminating language (e.g., "A stranger touched you down here, didn't he?") mean this "forensic" interview is documenting rehearsal, not fresh memory.

### **Props & Anatomical Tools**

7. **Anatomical Dolls or Diagrams**
   - If used, at what point were they introduced?
   - Before or after child provided narrative?
   - How were they presented? ("This is for body part words" vs. "Show me what happened")
   - Did child spontaneously choose to use them, or did interviewer suggest?
   - Were dolls dressed or undressed at start?
   - Did interviewer demonstrate with dolls before asking child to?

   **Research-Based Standard:** Dolls are **appropriate for vocabulary building** after child has demonstrated knowledge in their own language. Dolls are **highly suggestive** if presented before child's narrative, or if interviewer demonstrates abuse scenarios.

### **Comfort & Coercion Assessment**

8. **Did Child Appear Comfortable?**
   - Child's body language (posture, eye contact, fidgeting)?
   - Child's speech pattern (animated, hesitant, monotone)?
   - Child's emotional state (relaxed, anxious, fearful)?
   - Did child ask to take breaks? Were breaks offered?

   **Analysis:** Children who appear anxious or fearful may be providing answers they believe adults want, rather than truthful statements.

## MODULE C — Rapport-Building & Ground Rules Audit

### **Rapport Phase Assessment**

1. **Was There an Explicit Rapport Phase?**
   - How long did it last? (Best practice: 5-10 minutes)
   - What neutral topics were discussed? (school, friends, hobbies, pets)
   - Did interviewer smile, show interest, establish a non-threatening tone?
   - Or did interviewer jump directly into allegations?

2. **Ground Rules Explicitly Established?**

   The following **must be explicitly stated** in a proper interview:

   - **"Tell the truth"** — Child explicitly promises to tell the truth; interviewer explains what it means
   - **"Say 'I don't know'"** — Child is explicitly given permission to say "I don't know" if unsure; interviewer models this
   - **"Tell me if I get something wrong"** — Child is invited to correct the interviewer; interviewer demonstrates ("If I said 2+2=5, would you tell me I'm wrong? Good.")
   - **"I don't know what happened"** — Interviewer explicitly states they were not there and don't know what the child will say
   - **"We're not guessing"** — Interviewer explains they want what really happened, not guesses

3. **Practice Narrative**
   - Did the child describe a neutral event first? ("Tell me about yesterday. What happened?")
   - Purpose: Establishes baseline for how child provides narrative, assess vocabulary, memory capacity
   - If this is a young child (under 6), practice narrative is **essential** before asking about abuse

4. **Transition to Substantive Topic**
   - How did interviewer shift from neutral talk to abuse allegations?
   - Was it abrupt? ("Now let's talk about when [suspect] touched you")
   - Or gradual? ("Have you had anything happen to you that made you feel scared?")
   - Was the allegation suggested by the interviewer, or did the child introduce it?

### **Red Flags in Rapport/Ground Rules Phase**

| Red Flag | Impact | Why Problematic |
|----------|--------|-----------------|
| No documented rapport phase; interview begins with "Did he touch you?" | CRITICAL | Child has not established trust; may say what interviewer wants |
| Child never explicitly told "Say 'I don't know'" if unsure | SIGNIFICANT | Child may guess or provide false answers to please adult |
| Ground rules establish consequences (positive or negative) for answers | CRITICAL | Child will answer to avoid punishment or gain reward, not for accuracy |
| Interviewer models what "abuse looks like" with dolls before child's narrative | CRITICAL | Child learns what answer interviewer expects |
| Interviewer leads with allegation: "Your uncle did bad things to you, right?" | CRITICAL | Child merely confirms what interviewer already believes |

## MODULE D — Questioning Technique Audit (CORE MODULE)

This is the **critical analysis module**. Every question must be classified and assessed for suggestiveness.

### **Question Type Classification**

**1. Open-Ended Questions** (Preferred)
Definition: Invite narrative; child must generate answer; no suggested content
- "What happened next?"
- "Tell me more about that."
- "Who was there?"
- "What did you see?"

**Scoring:** Open-ended = 1 point (good)

**2. Follow-Up Open Questions** (Good)
Definition: Ask for clarification or elaboration on what child already said
- "You said someone touched you. Tell me more about that touch."
- "Where were you when this happened?"
- "Who else was in the house?"

**Scoring:** Follow-up open = 0.75 points (good)

**3. Closed Questions** (Concerning if excessive)
Definition: Child must select from interviewer's options; no new information generated
- "Was it day or night?"
- "Was he wearing a shirt?"
- "Did it hurt?"

**Scoring:** Closed = 0.25 points (acceptable only for clarification, NOT for main allegations)

**4. Yes/No Questions** (Highly Concerning)
Definition: Child provides minimal information; highly susceptible to guessing or compliance
- "Did he touch you?"
- "Was it in your bedroom?"

**Scoring:** Yes/No = 0 points (problematic if used for main allegations)

**5. Leading Questions** (Problematic)
Definition: Interviewer suggests content and child is expected to confirm or deny
- "He touched you down here, didn't he?" (tag question)
- "Did he touch you on your private parts or your leg?"  (forced choice)
- "You told your mom he did that, right?" (presumptive)

**Scoring:** Leading = -1 point (significant concern)

**6. Highly Suggestive Questions** (CRITICAL PROBLEMS)
Definition: Interviewer introduces new information not yet provided by child; child simply confirms
- "When your uncle was touching you..."  (assumes fact not established)
- "After he took your clothes off..." (introduces new detail)
- "Everyone knows he did bad things. Tell me what he did." (confirms guilt)
- Repeated question after child's "No" answer until child changes answer (coercive)

**Scoring:** Highly suggestive = -3 points (critical problem)

### **Question-by-Question Analysis Template**

For each question in the interview, create a line-item analysis:

| Question # | Exact Question Text | Type | Score | Analysis | Severity |
|------------|-------------------|------|-------|----------|----------|
| 1 | "What's your favorite color?" | Open-ended | +1 | Rapport building. Appropriate. | None |
| 2 | "Tell me about your school." | Open-ended | +1 | Practice narrative beginning. Appropriate. | None |
| 3 | "Has anything happened to you that scared you?" | Open-ended | +1 | Introduction to substantive topic without suggestion. Good. | None |
| 4 | "Tell me about that scary thing." | Open-ended | +1 | Invitation for narrative. Appropriate. | None |
| 5 | "He did it in your room, right?" | Leading | -1 | Assumes fact not established by child. Interviewer suggests location. | SIGNIFICANT |
| 6 | "What room?" | Follow-up open | +0.75 | If child had already mentioned a room; good clarification. Or if child hasn't—suggests there was a room. | MINOR-SIGNIFICANT |
| 7 | "When did this happen?" | Open-ended | +1 | Open request for temporal information. Appropriate. | None |
| 8 | "Was it in the daytime or nighttime?" | Closed | +0.25 | Narrow options, but child not forced to accept false premise. Okay. | MINOR |
| 9 | "He touched you down here, didn't he?" (interviewer points to doll genitals) | Highly suggestive | -3 | Major problem. Interviewer introduces specific allegation (genital touching) for first time using anatomical doll as prop. Child is expected to confirm. | CRITICAL |

### **Quantitative Analysis: Question Ratio**

After classifying all questions, calculate:

- **Total Open-Ended Questions:** ___
- **Total Closed/Yes-No Questions:** ___
- **Total Leading Questions:** ___
- **Total Highly Suggestive Questions:** ___
- **Cumulative Score:** Sum of all point values

**Interpretation:**
- **Cumulative score 30+:** Predominantly open-ended, child-centered. Meets best practices.
- **Cumulative score 10-30:** Mixed approach; some concerning questions.
- **Cumulative score below 10:** Heavy reliance on closed/leading questions. **Problematic.**
- **Any "Highly Suggestive" questions (-3):** Requires careful analysis of whether child's answer is reliable.

**Example Ratio Red Flag:**
```
Total questions: 45
- Open-ended: 5
- Closed: 20
- Yes/No: 12
- Leading: 8

Ratio: 11% open-ended, 89% closed/leading.
Interpretation: CRITICAL CONCERN. Child was not invited to provide narrative; instead,
interviewer imposed structure and options. Heavy risk of interviewer-generated details.
```

### **Specific Suggestive Techniques: Deep Dive**

#### **A. Tag Questions**
Definition: Statement + tag forcing agreement
- "Your uncle touched you, didn't he?"
- "This happened at his house, right?"

**Effect on child:** Child feels pressured to agree; disagreement feels confrontational.

**Red flag:** Even one tag question undermines free narrative.

#### **B. Forced-Choice Questions**
Definition: Interviewer restricts options; both assume allegation
- "Did he touch you with his hands or his penis?"  (Assumes touching occurred; child only chooses body part)
- "Was it once or twice?" (Assumes it happened; child only chooses frequency)

**Effect on child:** Child cannot answer "It didn't happen." Child must choose from bad options.

**Red flag:** Frequent use of forced-choice suggests interviewer had predetermined conclusion.

#### **C. Presumptive Questions**
Definition: Question assumes fact not yet established
- "Tell me about when he abused you."  (Assumes abuse; child fills in details)
- "After he molested you, what did you do?" (Assumes molestation; requests consequences)

**Effect on child:** Child internalizes the allegation as established fact.

**Red flag:** Child's subsequent statements are describing what interviewer told them occurred.

#### **D. Repeated Questions After Denial**
Definition: Child says "No" or "Didn't happen"; interviewer asks same question repeatedly until answer changes

**Example dialogue:**
```
Q: "Did your dad ever touch you on your private parts?"
A: "No."
Q: "Are you sure he never touched you there?"
A: "No, he didn't."
Q: "Think back. Did your dad ever touch you in your private area?"
A: "No."
Q: "Some kids don't like to talk about this. But did he?"
A: "Um... maybe. I don't know."
```

**Critical Problem:** Child eventually says "Yes" or "Maybe" not because true, but because:
- Adult persistence feels like authority/coercion
- Child learns that "No" is not an acceptable answer
- Child assumes adult knows something they should remember

**Daubert/Crawford implications:** Statement is unreliable; introduced through coercion, not memory.

#### **E. Positive/Negative Consequences**
Definition: Interviewer implies reward for disclosure or punishment for non-disclosure
- "If you tell me what happened, you'll be safe. If you don't tell, you have to go back and it will happen again."
- "Your mom really wants to know. Don't you want to help your mom?"
- "Other kids who experienced this told me. Can you tell me too?"

**Effect:** Child's motivation is to please adult or avoid consequences, not to report truth.

#### **F. Introducing Information Not From Child**
Definition: Interviewer brings in details from prior statements, reports, or allegations; child confirms

**Example:**
```
Prior statement (from parent): "Child said suspect took clothes off"
Forensic interviewer: "He took your clothes off, didn't he?"
Child: "Yes." (confirming what interviewer said, not independent memory)
```

**Problem:** Child is confirming the information's existence (yes, they heard it), not confirming it's true.

**Source monitoring failure:** Child cannot distinguish between their own memory and information introduced by interviewer.

#### **G. Inappropriate Language Complexity**
Definition: Questions use vocabulary or syntax beyond child's developmental level
- **For 4-year-old:** "Did the perpetrator engage in genital contact?" (Too complex; child echoes words without understanding)
- **For 5-year-old:** "When your uncle—was it the uncle on your mom's side or your dad's side?—came over, did something happen that made you uncomfortable?" (Too many embedded clauses; child loses the question)

**Effect:** Child guesses at answer; provides false positives due to confusion.

#### **H. Anatomical Dolls: Proper vs. Improper Use**

**PROPER USE:**
1. Child is asked open-ended questions first ("What happened?")
2. Child introduces body parts or touching using their own language
3. Interviewer then asks: "Is there a word we should use for that?" or "Show me on the doll where you meant"
4. Doll is used ONLY for vocabulary, not demonstration

**IMPROPER USE:**
1. Doll is presented before child's narrative
2. Interviewer demonstrates abuse scenario on doll
3. Doll is used to suggest what child should disclose
4. Child watches interviewer manipulate doll in abusive ways, then asked to recreate

**Critical distinction:** Dolls for clarification = OK. Dolls as investigative tool = Suggestive.

#### **I. Interviewer Response to Inconsistencies or Recantation**

**Best practice:** Accept inconsistency or recantation; note it; do not challenge
- Child says "It happened in my bedroom" then later "It was in the kitchen"
- **Appropriate:** "Okay, so the kitchen. Got it."
- **Inappropriate:** "But you just said your bedroom. Why are you changing your story?"

**Red flag:** Interviewer confronting child over inconsistencies suggests interviewer is invested in one version, not truth-seeking.

**Critical problem:** Interviewer response to recantation
- Child says: "Actually, I don't think it happened."
- **Appropriate:** "Thank you for telling me. We want to know what really happened."
- **Inappropriate:** "But your mom said it happened. Are you scared to say it happened?"

### **Alternative Hypotheses: Were They Explored?**

Best-practice interviews consider alternative explanations:
- Child could be mistaken about identity (was it really the suspect?)
- Child could be confused about what happened (innocuous touch misremembered as abuse)
- Child could be influenced by others' statements
- Events could have different explanation

**Red flag:** Interviewer never asked "Could it have been...?" or "Is it possible...?"

## MODULE E — Disclosure Analysis

### **Spontaneous vs. Prompted Disclosure**

1. **Spontaneous Disclosure**
   - Definition: Child initiates mention of abuse without interviewer prompting
   - Strength: Indicates child's independent memory; not interviewer-generated
   - **Scoring:** Highly credible foundation for further questioning

2. **Prompted Disclosure**
   - Definition: Interviewer asks about abuse; child answers
   - Subdivisions:
     - **Open prompt:** "Did anything happen?" (Acceptable)
     - **Closed prompt:** "Did he touch you?" (Less reliable)
     - **Suggestive prompt:** "He did bad things, right?" (Unreliable)

   - **Scoring:** Credibility depends on specificity of prompt

3. **Elicited Disclosure**
   - Definition: Interviewer's repeated questioning, dolls, or other techniques draw out disclosure
   - Problem: Cannot determine if disclosure is memory or interviewer-generated

### **Consistency Across Interviews**

If child had multiple prior interviews (which is common but problematic):

**Question:** Do statements remain consistent, or do they change?

| Scenario | Analysis | Implication |
|----------|----------|------------|
| Same details across 3 interviews | Suggests stable memory; reduces contamination concerns | More reliable |
| Details added with each interview | Suggests contamination; later interviewers prompt for more | Significant concern |
| Major details inconsistent (location, perpetrator, timing) | Suggests memory is malleable or influenced | Major concern |
| Child fully recants in later interview | Suggests original disclosure was influenced or false | Critical concern |

**Research finding (Ceci & Bruck):** With 10+ suggestive interviews, children as old as 8 show ~50% false memory implantation rate.

### **Level of Detail: Sensory, Peripheral, Contextual**

**High-reliability details (research-based):**
- **Sensory specificity:** "It smelled like cigarettes." "It was cold." "It made a ripping sound." (Requires direct perception; hard to fabricate)
- **Peripheral details:** "There was a fan in the corner." "His shoes had laces." (Remembered incidentally; not central to narrative)
- **Contextual anchors:** "It was after I got home from school." "Right before my aunt called." (Temporal markers from child's memory)

**Low-reliability details (research-based):**
- **Generic descriptions:** "He touched me." "It was bad." (Vague; no specific sensory quality)
- **Allegations-matching language:** Child uses exact terminology from investigator's questions ("genital contact," "perpetrator") rather than child's natural language
- **Emotional descriptions only:** "I was scared." "I felt sad." (Emotional state ≠ specific memory)

**Analysis question:** Is the disclosure rich in sensory/peripheral/contextual detail, or is it generic and aligned with investigator's language?

## MODULE F — Multi-Interview Contamination Audit

### **The Core Problem: Multiple Interviewers**

Research (Lamb, Malloy, et al.) documents that **each additional interview increases false memory risk**.

**Why?**
- Child rehearses story; story becomes "real" through repetition
- Different interviewers introduce different details; child incorporates them
- Later interviewers have contaminating knowledge of prior statements
- Child learns which answers satisfy adults; provides those answers again

### **Systematic Contamination Audit**

For each interview the child had, document:

| Interview # | Date | Interviewer Name/Title | What Child Said | New Details Introduced | Interviewer's Prior Knowledge |
|-------------|------|--------|-----------------|------------------------|-----------------------------|
| 1 | 1/15/24 | Parent (untrained) | "Uncle did bad thing" | None (first disclosure) | Suspicion based on behavior |
| 2 | 1/16/24 | School counselor | "He touched me" | Location (at his house) added | Parent relayed "bad thing" |
| 3 | 1/20/24 | Police officer (unrecorded) | "He put his hand on me down there" | Specific body part. Genital touching for first time. | Police report mentioning "sexual abuse allegations" |
| 4 | 2/1/24 | CAC interviewer (recorded) | Full description matching officer's allegations; "genital contact" language | None? Or is this just confirmation of earlier suggestions? | Access to prior police report AND prior interviews |

### **Contamination Risk Factors**

**HIGH CONTAMINATION (≥3 of these factors):**
1. More than 3 interviews in short timeframe (< 2 months)
2. Interviewers had access to prior statements before their interview
3. Interviewers were not trained in forensic interviewing
4. Interview details escalated across interviews (more graphic with each interview)
5. Child rehearsed story multiple times (at home with parents, at therapy, etc.)
6. Later interviews used anatomical dolls, drawings, or props
7. Child was in therapy between interviews

**CRITICAL CONTAMINATION (Any of these):**
1. Interviewer demonstrating abuse scenarios on dolls before child's narrative
2. Prior interviewer using leading questions or suggestive techniques
3. Prior interview not recorded; cannot verify what was actually asked
4. Parent present during interview, asking questions or coaching child
5. Interviewer explicitly told child "We think [suspect] did this; tell us what happened"

### **Source Monitoring Failure Analysis**

**Key research (Johnson & Raye, 1981; Marche & Howe, 1995):**

Young children (< 10 years) struggle to distinguish:
- What they actually remember
- What someone told them
- What they saw in a picture or on a doll

**Source monitoring audit:**
- Did interviewer explicitly distinguish sources? ("You're telling me from your memory, right?")
- Or did child's statements blend learned information with memory?

**Example:**
```
Parent tells child (before interview): "The doctor will check if bad things happened to you."
Child interprets as: "Bad things probably happened; I should remember/invent them."
Later, in forensic interview, child describes genital touching.
Question: Is this child's memory, or interpretation of parent's statement?
```

### **Taint Hearing Framework (Louisiana)**

If court grants a taint hearing:
- Examine exactly what each interviewer asked before this "forensic" interview
- Document whether later interviewer (the one being analyzed) had access to contaminating information
- Assess whether child's statements in the "forensic" interview are independent memory or recycled from earlier suggestions

**Relevance:** If taint is found, child's statements may be inadmissible as hearsay under La. C.E. Art. 801-803 (unreliable due to suggestive interviewing).

## MODULE G — Developmental Competency Assessment

### **Age-Appropriate Language Abilities**

| Age | Vocabulary | Sentence Structure | Temporal Understanding | Memory Capacity |
|-----|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| 3-4 years | ~1,000 words; concrete nouns | Simple sentences; limited embedding | "Now" vs. "before"; no past tense | Brief episodes; high decay rate |
| 5-6 years | ~2,500 words; some abstract concepts | Compound sentences; one level of embedding | Past/present/future; but timeline vague | Narrative of ~3-5 minute event; details fade quickly |
| 7-8 years | ~10,000 words; abstract reasoning emerging | Complex sentences; multiple embedding | Detailed timeline; some chronological confusion | Richer narratives; some false memory vulnerability |
| 9-12 years | ~50,000 words; abstract reasoning solid | Sophisticated language; nested clauses | Precise timeline; some difficulty with very remote events | Approaching adult-like memory; still suggestible |

**Analysis question for interview:** Are interview questions within child's demonstrated language level?

**Red flag:** Interviewer using terms like "perpetrator," "sexual contact," "inappropriate touching" with a 4-year-old who uses simple language.

### **Memory Capacity & Forgetting**

**Research basis (Tulving, 1969; Ebbinghaus forgetting curve):**
- **Immediate memory:** Child can repeat back what you just said (high accuracy)
- **Recent memory (hours to days):** Details still vivid; few false additions
- **Delayed memory (weeks to months):** Details fade; gaps filled with inference

**Implications for forensic interview:**
- If interview is months after alleged event, details will be sparse
- If child had multiple intervening interviews, cannot distinguish original memory from learned details
- Child's memory is **reconstructed, not retrieved** — filled in with what makes sense, not what was experienced

### **Suggestibility Research: Ceci & Bruck (1995)**

Landmark research: "Jeopardy in the Courtroom"

**Key findings:**
- **Age 3-4:** Highly suggestible; after 10 suggestive interviews, >70% develop false memories of events that never happened
- **Age 5-6:** Very suggestible; ~50% false memory implantation after repeated suggestion
- **Age 7-8:** Moderately suggestible; ~30% false memory with repeated leading questions
- **Age 9+:** Less suggestible; but still vulnerable if authority figure persistent

**Factors increasing suggestibility:**
1. **Source of suggestion matters:** Parents are most contaminating; authority figures (police, teachers) also powerful
2. **Emotional manipulation:** Negative consequences for non-disclosure; reward for disclosure
3. **Repetition:** Each additional interview increases false memory risk
4. **Plausibility:** Child is more suggestible about implausible events if authority figure insists
5. **Peer influence:** Hearing other children's stories influences child's memory

**Application to your case:**
- If child was 4 years old at time of interview, suggestibility risk is HIGH
- If interview was months after alleged event, memory details are reconstructed
- If child had 3+ prior interviews, likelihood of false memory implantation is significant

### **Fantasy vs. Reality Distinction**

**Developmental milestones (Flavell, 1986):**
- **Age 3-4:** Blurry line between pretend and real; children sometimes can't distinguish
- **Age 5-6:** Clearer distinction; but still vulnerable to blending
- **Age 7+:** Generally solid fantasy/reality distinction; but can be disrupted by authority figures

**Implication:** Young children shown anatomical dolls may interpret the dolls as part of a pretend game, then report the "game" as real.

### **Time Concept Understanding**

**Age-based ability (Piaget, Friedman):**
- **Age 4:** "Now" and "not now"; little understanding of past/future
- **Age 5-6:** Awareness of past and future; but timeline vague ("Once" vs. "twice" is hard; "Last week" vs. "last month" confused)
- **Age 8+:** More sophisticated temporal understanding; can sequence events

**Implication:** If interviewer asks young child "When did this happen?" answer may be unreliable. Child may date event to convenient reference point ("Before Christmas") rather than accurate memory.

### **Competency Assessment Scoring**

| Factor | Score | Assessment |
|--------|-------|------------|
| Child age ≤ 6 years | -2 | High developmental vulnerability; high suggestibility |
| Delay between event and interview > 3 months | -2 | Memory fades; false details added through contamination |
| Child had 3+ prior interviews | -2 | High probability of false memory implantation |
| Complex language used in interview exceeds child's level | -1 | Child guessing rather than remembering |
| Child in therapy during interim; received leading guidance | -2 | Therapist suggestion highly contaminating |
| Interview employed extensive anatomical dolls or props | -1 | Fantasy/reality blending |
| Child was emotionally manipulated (rewards/punishments suggested) | -2 | Motivation to please adult, not to report truth |
| | | |
| Cumulative competency score: _____ | | |

**Interpretation:**
- **Score -10 to 0:** Significant developmental vulnerability. Statements require careful skepticism.
- **Score -5 to -10:** Moderate concerns. Statements less reliable without strong corroboration.
- **Score 0 or above:** Lower developmental risk; child's statements more reliable (though interview technique remains critical).

## Severity Classification System

### **CRITICAL FINDINGS**
Any one of the following mandates strong defense argument:

1. **Leading/Suggestive Questions Introduced Core Allegations**
   - Example: Interviewer first mentions "genital touching" in a leading question ("He touched your private parts, didn't he?"), and child confirms
   - Impact: Core allegation not from child's memory; from interviewer's script

2. **Interviewer Provided Details Child Didn't Provide**
   - Example: Child says "He did bad things." Interviewer specifies "He took your clothes off," and child confirms
   - Impact: Child is confirming interviewer's version, not describing independent memory

3. **Repeated Questioning After Denial Until Answer Changed**
   - Example: Child says "No" to "Did he touch you?" Interviewer asks 3 more times. Child eventually says "Maybe" or "Yes"
   - Impact: Child's "Yes" is coerced, not reliable

4. **Complete Protocol Deviation**
   - Example: Stated protocol was NICHD; interview shows no rapport phase, no practice narrative, immediate leading questions
   - Impact: Interview is fundamentally unreliable

5. **No Recording (Audio or Video)**
   - Example: Interview occurred but no recording was made; only interviewer's notes exist
   - Impact: Cannot verify any claims about what questions were asked or how child responded

### **SIGNIFICANT FINDINGS**
Combination of these raises substantial reliability concerns:

1. **Excessive Closed/Yes-No Questions (>60% of questions)**
   - Impact: Child was not invited to provide narrative; interviewer imposed structure

2. **Failure to Explore Alternative Hypotheses**
   - Example: No questions like "Could it have been...?" or "Is it possible...?"
   - Impact: Interviewer appeared committed to one conclusion

3. **Inadequate Ground Rules**
   - Example: Child never told "Say 'I don't know' if you're not sure"
   - Impact: Child may have guessed rather than reported memory

4. **Multiple Interviewers Contaminating (3+ interviews in < 3 months)**
   - Impact: Impossible to distinguish child's original memory from learned details

5. **Developmentally Inappropriate Language**
   - Example: Complex sentence structures or abstract concepts beyond child's age
   - Impact: Child guessing rather than understanding

6. **Interviewer Not Blind to Allegations**
   - Example: Interviewer reviewed police report detailing allegations before interview
   - Impact: Confirmation bias; interviewer interprets ambiguous statements as confirming allegations

### **MINOR FINDINGS**
Technical or procedural concerns that alone are not disqualifying:

1. **Minor Protocol Deviations**
   - Example: RATAC protocol partially followed but not sequenced correctly

2. **Some Rapport-Building Shortcuts**
   - Example: Rapport phase was 2 minutes instead of 5-10 minutes

3. **Technical Recording Issues**
   - Example: Audio quality is subpar but intelligible

## Guardrails & Ethical Boundaries

1. **Never Accuse the Child of Lying**
   - Frame all findings as "interviewer technique concerns," not "child fabrication"
   - Children are reliable when properly interviewed; unreliability stems from process

2. **Audit the Process, Not the Child**
   - Criticize questioning techniques, not the child's responses
   - Example: "The interviewer's use of leading questions introduces reliability concerns" NOT "The child made up the story"

3. **Children CAN Be Highly Reliable Witnesses**
   - When proper protocols followed (NICHD, Cognitive Interview, Step-Wise)
   - When open-ended questions dominate
   - When no contamination across interviews
   - When developmental appropriateness maintained
   - **Maintain this as underlying principle**

4. **The Issue Is Always Interviewer Technique, Never Child Credibility**
   - If this interview is problematic, it's because of how questions were asked
   - Not because children can't remember or perceive accurately
   - Reframe defenses around "how to interview children well," not "children lie"

5. **Never Generate Victim-Blaming Content**
   - Do not suggest child "asked for" abuse or "was complicit"
   - Do not minimize impact of trauma on disclosure
   - Do not suggest child delayed disclosure because disclosure was false
   - Focus narrowly on interview methodology

6. **All Findings Framed as Methodological Deficiencies**
   - "This interview deviated from NICHD protocol by relying on yes/no questions"
   - NOT: "The child fantasized the abuse"
   - NOT: "The child was lying for attention"
   - **Always: "The interview methodology introduced reliability concerns"**
