## MODULE D -- Estimator Variable Analysis

Estimator variables are factors not under law enforcement control that affect the witness's ability to perceive, remember, and accurately identify the perpetrator. Law enforcement cannot control these factors, but these factors profoundly affect identification reliability. The defense must present them to the court and jury.

### D.1 -- Perception Conditions (Encoding Phase)

The reliability of any identification begins with what the witness actually perceived during the crime:

**Lighting:**
| Condition | Risk Level | Scientific Basis |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Full daylight, face illuminated | Low | Adequate for facial feature encoding |
| Indoor artificial lighting (bright) | Low-Moderate | Depends on light type; fluorescent can distort color perception |
| Dusk / dawn / dim interior | Moderate-High | Reduced visual acuity; facial features less distinguishable |
| Streetlight at distance | High | Streetlights create shadows, color distortion; insufficient for reliable facial encoding at distance |
| Darkness / no illumination | Very High | Facial identification in darkness is scientifically unsupportable; visual system cannot encode facial features without adequate light |

**Distance:**
| Distance | Risk Level | Scientific Basis |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| 0-15 feet (conversational) | Low | Within range for reliable facial feature perception |
| 15-50 feet | Moderate | Depends on lighting and duration; facial features less distinct |
| 50-150 feet | High | Visual acuity limits facial feature discrimination at this range; most witnesses cannot reliably identify facial features beyond ~75 feet |
| 150+ feet | Very High | Identification at this distance is scientifically unsupportable under most conditions |

**Duration of Observation:**
| Duration | Risk Level | Scientific Basis |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Extended (minutes) | Low | Sufficient time for facial feature encoding |
| Moderate (30 sec - 2 min) | Moderate | May be sufficient if other conditions favorable |
| Brief (5-30 seconds) | High | Limited encoding time; witness may have encoded only general features (race, build, clothing) rather than distinguishing facial characteristics |
| Fleeting (under 5 seconds) | Very High | Insufficient for reliable facial feature encoding; identification depends on general impressions, not specific features |

**Obstructions:**
Evaluate any factor that partially or fully obscured the perpetrator's face: hats, hoodies, sunglasses, masks, facial hair changes, turned head or profile view, objects between witness and perpetrator.

### D.2 -- Witness Factors (Encoding and Retrieval Phases)

**Stress and Arousal:**
The relationship between stress and memory is described by the *Yerkes-Dodson Law* -- moderate stress can enhance attention to central details but impairs encoding of peripheral details, including facial features. High stress, particularly fear for one's life, significantly impairs encoding.

> **Deffenbacher et al. (2004) meta-analysis:** High stress negatively affects both the accuracy of facial identification and the accuracy of recall of crime details. The effect is robust and has been replicated across multiple studies.

**Weapon Focus Effect:**
When a weapon is present, the witness's attention is drawn to the weapon and away from the perpetrator's face. This is one of the most well-established findings in eyewitness science:

> **Steblay (1992) meta-analysis:** The presence of a weapon significantly reduces the accuracy of eyewitness identification. The effect is stronger when the weapon is unusual or unexpected and when it is directly threatening the witness.

> **Fawcett et al. (2013):** Updated meta-analysis confirming the weapon focus effect across diverse study designs.

**Cross-Racial Identification (Own-Race Bias / Other-Race Effect):**
People are significantly worse at identifying faces of individuals from a different racial or ethnic group than their own:

> **Meissner & Brigham (2001) meta-analysis:** The cross-race effect produces a 1.4x increase in false identifications and a 1.56x decrease in correct identifications when the witness and perpetrator are of different races. The effect is consistent across studies and robust.

> **NAS Report (2014) at 96:** "Research has consistently demonstrated the own-race bias in facial recognition."

**If the identification in the case is cross-racial, this is a SIGNIFICANT estimator variable deficiency that must be flagged and analyzed.**

**Witness Age:**
- Very young witnesses (under 12) and elderly witnesses (over 65) show reduced accuracy in identification tasks.
- Young children are more susceptible to suggestion and may have difficulty distinguishing between memory and suggestion.
- Elderly witnesses may have reduced visual acuity and slower cognitive processing.

**Intoxication:**
Alcohol and drug intoxication at the time of the crime impair encoding. The greater the intoxication, the greater the impairment:

> **Hagsand et al. (2013):** Intoxicated witnesses show reduced accuracy in lineup identification tasks, particularly at higher blood alcohol levels.

### D.3 -- Memory Factors (Retention Phase)

**Retention Interval:**
Memory degrades over time. The most significant memory loss occurs in the first 24-48 hours after encoding (Ebbinghaus forgetting curve). The longer the interval between the crime and the identification procedure, the less reliable the identification:

| Interval | Risk Level | Note |
|----------|-----------|------|
| Same day (within hours) | Lower | Memory still relatively fresh; but note that even fresh memories can be wrong |
| 1-7 days | Moderate | Significant decay has occurred; facial memory is particularly susceptible |
| 1-4 weeks | High | Substantial memory degradation; identification increasingly unreliable |
| Months | Very High | Memory significantly degraded; identification is largely based on reconstruction rather than recognition |
| Years | Extreme | Identification has minimal scientific support as a reliable act of recognition |

**Post-Event Information:**
The witness's memory is not a fixed recording. It is subject to alteration by post-event information -- anything the witness learns about the crime or suspect after the event:

- **Co-witness discussion:** Witnesses who discuss the crime with each other contaminate each other's memories. Details from one witness's account can be incorporated into another's memory (Gabbert et al., 2003).
- **Media exposure:** News coverage, social media posts, and photographs of the suspect can alter the witness's memory of the perpetrator.
- **Law enforcement comments:** Any information provided by officers before the identification procedure -- descriptions of the suspect, case details, expressions of confidence -- can contaminate the witness's memory.
- **Mugshot exposure:** If the witness viewed mugshots before the formal identification procedure, the witness may be identifying the person whose mugshot they saw rather than the person they saw during the crime (the *mugshot commitment effect* / *unconscious transference*).
