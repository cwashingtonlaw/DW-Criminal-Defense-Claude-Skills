# Drug Lab Methodology Reference
## Field Testing vs. Confirmatory Analysis

---

## CRITICAL DISTINCTION: FIELD TEST vs. LAB CONFIRMATION

### Field Tests (PRELIMINARY, NOT CONCLUSIVE)

Field tests are preliminary screening tools used during or immediately after drug seizure. They are NOT conclusive evidence of drug identity.

**Common reagent tests:**
- **Marquis reagent** — Color change for opiates, MDMA, methamphetamine, amphetamine
- **Mandelin reagent** — Color change for MDMA, ketamine, amphetamine
- **Mecke reagent** — Color change for cocaine, heroin, opiates
- **Scott reagent** — Color change for cocaine
- **Froehde reagent** — Color change for opiates, amphetamine

**How they work:** Reagent is applied to small sample of suspected substance. Chemical reaction produces color change (blue, pink, yellow, etc.) characteristic of certain drugs.

**Critical limitations:**
- Many non-drug substances produce identical or similar color changes
- False positive rate is high (15-30% reported in some studies)
- False positives documented for: chocolate, vitamins, caffeine, baking soda, vitamins, herbal supplements, some legal medications

**Examples of false positives:**
- Chocolate triggers false positive on some opiate tests
- Vitamins (especially B vitamins) trigger false positives on cocaine tests
- Baking soda can trigger color reactions
- Some legal supplements trigger MDMA-like reactions

**Admissibility:** Field test results are often admissible to establish probable cause for arrest, but are NOT sufficient to establish corpus delicti (the body of the crime — actual drug) for prosecution.

**Your defense strategy:** If prosecution's only evidence is a field test (no lab confirmation), demand confirmatory testing before trial. Without lab confirmation, simple possession charge is significantly weakened.

## CONFIRMATORY LAB TESTING (GOLD STANDARD)

### GC-MS (Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry)

**Status:** Gold standard for drug identification. Used in virtually all legitimate drug labs.

**Methodology:**
1. Sample is dissolved in solvent
2. Sample is injected into gas chromatograph (heated to vaporize)
3. Vaporized sample travels through chromatography column
4. Different compounds separate based on molecular properties (boiling point, polarity)
5. Separated compounds enter mass spectrometer
6. Mass spectrometer fragments molecules and measures mass-to-charge ratio
7. Fragmentation pattern ("mass spectrum") is unique to each drug
8. Retention time (how long compound takes to travel through column) + mass spectrum = drug identification

**Advantages:**
- Separates mixture components individually
- Provides unique fragmentation pattern for each drug
- Can identify purity and presence of adulterants/cutting agents
- Highly specific (low false positive rate if properly operated)
- Can quantify amount of each component

**Retention time critical:** Each drug has known retention time. Example: cocaine's GC retention time is ~5.2 minutes; if sample shows peak at 5.2 minutes with cocaine's characteristic mass spectrum, identification is solid.

**Audit points for defense:**
- [ ] Were reference standards (known cocaine, known heroin samples) run before and after defendant's sample?
- [ ] Were retention times documented and matched to standards?
- [ ] Was mass spectrum characteristic of the identified drug?
- [ ] Were there contamination issues or carry-over from previous samples?
- [ ] Is the instrument calibrated and maintained per manufacturer specs?
- [ ] When was instrument last calibrated?

---

### HPLC (High-Performance Liquid Chromatography)

**Status:** Alternative to GC-MS, commonly used for heat-sensitive substances or liquid samples.

**When used:** Liquid samples (drug solutions, cough syrups), some powders that degrade with heating.

**Methodology:**
1. Sample dissolved in mobile phase (liquid solvent)
2. Sample is injected into HPLC column
3. Compounds separate based on interaction with column material
4. Separated compounds pass through detector (UV absorbance, fluorescence, mass spectrometry)
5. Detector produces signal proportional to amount of each compound

**Advantages:**
- Doesn't require vaporization (better for heat-sensitive drugs)
- Works for liquid and solid samples
- Can be paired with mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) for enhanced specificity

**Audit points for defense:**
- [ ] Was HPLC calibrated with appropriate reference standards?
- [ ] What detector was used (UV, MS, other)? MS is more specific.
- [ ] Were retention times documented?
- [ ] Were peak areas integrated correctly?
- [ ] Was there baseline resolution between drug and potential interferents?

## WEIGHT DETERMINATION METHODOLOGY

**Critical importance:** Weight thresholds determine charge tier and mandatory minimums.

### Tare Weight Protocol

**Tare weight = weight of container ALONE (without substance)**

**Net weight = gross weight − tare weight**

**Audit checklist:**
- [ ] Were containers weighed empty (tared) before substance was placed in them?
- [ ] Is tare weight documented in lab notes?
- [ ] What was total weight of container + substance (gross weight)?
- [ ] Is net weight calculated correctly?

**Example:** Bag weighs 2g empty. Bag + cocaine weighs 30g. Net cocaine weight = 30g − 2g = 28g.
- If prosecution charges based on 30g gross weight, defendant faces 10-year mandatory minimum
- If net weight is 28g, same minimum applies, but calculation is legally correct

**Defense angle:** If lab didn't properly tare containers, challenge the weight findings.

### Moisture Content and Purity

**For some substances (heroin, some pills), moisture content can inflate weight:**
- Substance may absorb water during storage
- Lab may or may not dry sample before final weighing
- If weight threshold depends on moisture-laden sample, argue for moisture adjustment

**Purity analysis (for mixtures):**
- If substance is cut with levamisole, filler, or other agents, lab should determine pure drug weight
- Prosecution may charge based on mixture weight (heavier)
- Defense argues pure drug weight applies to penalties

**Example:** 50g cocaine + levamisole mixture. Pure cocaine = 35g.
- Charge based on mixture (50g) = 20-year mandatory minimum (200g threshold not reached)
- But pure cocaine (35g) is more legally sound weight for penalties

## LAB ACCREDITATION AND ANALYST QUALIFICATION

### Lab Accreditation

**ASCLD/LAB (American Society of Crime Laboratory Directors/Laboratory Accreditation Board):**
- Gold standard accreditation for forensic labs
- Requires specific procedures, quality control, proficiency testing
- Labs not accredited may have less rigorous standards

**Questions to ask:**
- [ ] Is the lab ASCLD/LAB accredited?
- [ ] What is the lab's accreditation status and date of last renewal?
- [ ] If not accredited, why not?

**ISO/IEC 17025:** International standard for lab accreditation. Some labs use ISO instead of ASCLD/LAB.

### Analyst Qualification

**Key questions for cross-examination:**
- [ ] How long has the analyst been analyzing drug samples?
- [ ] How many samples has the analyst analyzed in their career?
- [ ] What formal education does the analyst have? (Chemistry degree? Toxicology certification?)
- [ ] How many times has the analyst testified in court?
- [ ] Has the analyst ever had a case overturned or challenged due to methodology?
- [ ] Is the analyst certified by professional organizations (AAFS, etc.)?
- [ ] Any disciplinary records or reliability issues?

**Red flags:**
- Analyst with minimal experience
- Analyst testifying infrequently (lack of cross-examination experience)
- Analyst with prior findings that were questioned or overturned
- Lack of formal education in chemistry or toxicology

**Route to dw-expert-witness-evaluator** if analyst credentials are questionable.

## CHAIN OF CUSTODY AUDIT

Chain of custody is the documented record of evidence handling from seizure through lab analysis.

**Critical chain of custody elements:**
1. Initial seizure by officer: date, time, location, officer name
2. Bagging/sealing: how was substance secured? Sealed how?
3. Transportation to lab: who transported? When? How?
4. Lab intake: date/time received, condition of seal (intact or broken?)
5. Lab analysis: who performed analysis, when, what method?
6. Lab storage: how was substance stored between tests?
7. Final disposition: where is substance now? Can it be retested?

**Audit checklist:**
- [ ] Is chain of custody documented for EVERY transfer?
- [ ] Are dates and times complete?
- [ ] Are officer/analyst names legible?
- [ ] Were containers sealed properly at each transfer?
- [ ] Any gaps in custody (days missing, person unnamed, unsealed container)?
- [ ] If seal was broken, was evidence resealed and documented?

**Defense angle:** Breaks in chain of custody (unsealed container, missing documentation, unclear transfers) can support suppression or challenge evidence integrity.

## COMMON LAB METHODOLOGY CHALLENGES

### Instrument Contamination

**Cross-contamination:** Sample residue from previous analysis contaminates current sample.

**Defense questions:**
- [ ] How does lab clean instruments between samples?
- [ ] Are cleaning procedures documented?
- [ ] What frequency of instrument cleaning is required?
- [ ] Are reference blanks run (empty samples) to detect contamination?

### Retention Time Drift

**Issue:** Instrument calibration drifts over time, causing retention time to shift.

**Defense questions:**
- [ ] Were reference standards run on same day as defendant's sample?
- [ ] Did retention times match between reference standards and defendant's sample?
- [ ] If times drifted, was sample recalibrated or reanalyzed?

### Analyst Bias

**Issue:** Analyst's knowledge that substance is suspected "drug" may bias interpretation.

**Defense questions:**
- [ ] Did analyst know what substance was suspected before analysis?
- [ ] Are blind samples used (samples without label, analyst doesn't know identity)?
- [ ] Is GC-MS interpretation computerized or manual?

### Incomplete Spectrum Analysis

**Issue:** Lab reports only fragment ions consistent with drug, omits ions inconsistent with drug (cherry-picking).

**Defense angle:** Request COMPLETE mass spectrum printout (all ions, not just selected ones). May show evidence of other compounds.

## RE-TESTING AND INDEPENDENT LAB ANALYSIS

### Right to Retest

**Statutory right:** Defendant generally has right to independent lab analysis at defendant's expense.

**Practical considerations:**
- Is remaining sample sufficient for retest?
- Is substance still usable (not degraded, not contaminated)?
- Cost of independent lab analysis: $500–$2,000+ (defense must pay unless indigent)
- Strategic value: Does retest help or hurt?

**When to pursue independent testing:**
- Prosecution's lab analysis is weak or ambiguous
- Substance identity is close call (similar retention times)
- Purity/mixture composition critical to defense
- Lab methodology is questionable

### Strategic Value Analysis

**Retest benefits:**
- Independent confirmation or contradiction of prosecution's results
- Expert witness with own test results (stronger credibility)
- Discovery of contamination or mixture not identified by state lab

**Retest risks:**
- If independent test confirms prosecution's result, you've just corroborated them
- Cost to defense client
- May not change outcome if prosecution's result is solid

**Decision:** Retest if there is genuine question about lab methodology or substance identity. Don't retest if prosecution's analysis is straightforward and solid.

## DAUBERT CHALLENGE TO LAB METHODOLOGY

If lab methodology is questionable, consider Daubert motion (La. C.E. Art. 702 equivalent) to exclude or challenge expert testimony.

**Daubert factors (federal standard, applied in Louisiana):**
1. Is methodology testable (falsifiable)?
2. Has methodology been subjected to peer review and publication?
3. What is the known or potential error rate?
4. What standards/protocols control the technique's operation?
5. Is methodology generally accepted in relevant scientific community?

**Example Daubert challenge:** Drug dog alert without proper handler training, certified training records, or documented accuracy rates = may challenge admissibility of alert.

- [ ] Lab is accredited (ASCLD/LAB or ISO)?
- [ ] GC-MS or HPLC used (confirmatory method)?
- [ ] Analyst qualifications documented (education, experience, prior testimony)?
- [ ] Retention times documented and match standards?
- [ ] Mass spectrum characteristic of identified drug?
- [ ] Reference standards run before/after defendant's sample?
- [ ] Chain of custody complete and unbroken?

- [ ] Mixture composition analyzed (pure drug weight identified separately)?

- [ ] Lab notes complete and legible?
- [ ] Remaining sample available for independent testing?
