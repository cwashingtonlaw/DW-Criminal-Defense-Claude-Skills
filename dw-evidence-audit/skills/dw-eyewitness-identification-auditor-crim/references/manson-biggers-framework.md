## MODULE F -- Manson/Brathwaite Reliability Challenge

The constitutional framework for challenging eyewitness identifications under the Due Process Clause is established by Manson v. Brathwaite, 432 U.S. 98 (1977), and Neil v. Biggers, 409 U.S. 188 (1972). The test is a two-step inquiry:

**Step 1:** Was the identification procedure impermissibly suggestive?
**Step 2:** If so, was the identification nevertheless reliable under the totality of the circumstances?

If the procedure was suggestive, the court evaluates reliability using the five Biggers/Manson factors. The identification is admissible only if reliability outweighs the corrupting effect of the suggestive procedure.

### F.1 -- The Five Reliability Factors

For each identification in the case, perform a factor-by-factor analysis with supporting science:

**Factor 1: The Opportunity to View the Criminal at the Time of the Crime**

| Sub-Factor | Assessment | Supporting Science |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| Lighting conditions | [Evaluate] | Vision science on luminance thresholds for face perception |
| Distance from perpetrator | [Evaluate] | Visual acuity limits; Loftus & Harley (2005) |
| Duration of observation | [Evaluate] | Encoding time research; Memon et al. (2003) |
| Obstructions to view | [Evaluate] | Partial occlusion effects on face recognition |
| Angle of view (frontal vs. profile) | [Evaluate] | Face recognition is significantly impaired for non-frontal views |

**Factor 2: The Witness's Degree of Attention**

| Sub-Factor | Assessment | Supporting Science |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| Was the witness a victim or bystander? | [Evaluate] | Victims under threat have divided attention |
| Was a weapon present? | [Evaluate] | Weapon focus effect: Steblay (1992) |
| Was the witness under stress? | [Evaluate] | Yerkes-Dodson Law; Deffenbacher et al. (2004) |
| Did the witness have reason to pay attention to the face? | [Evaluate] | Intentional vs. incidental encoding |
| Was the witness intoxicated? | [Evaluate] | Alcohol impairs encoding: Hagsand et al. (2013) |

**Factor 3: The Accuracy of the Witness's Prior Description**

| Sub-Factor | Assessment | Supporting Science |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| Was a description given before any identification procedure? | [Evaluate] | Description must predate identification |
| How detailed was the description? | [Evaluate] | Sparse descriptions (e.g., "tall Black male") provide weak evidence of recognition ability |
| Does the description match the defendant? | [Evaluate] | Discrepancies between description and defendant are significant |
| Does the description match the fillers equally well? | [Evaluate] | If yes, the description does not distinguish the defendant from the fillers |
| Does the description contain any incorrect features? | [Evaluate] | Incorrect features suggest poor encoding |

> **Critical defense point:** The witness's *description* accuracy is not the same as the witness's *identification* accuracy. A witness can give a general description that matches many people and still make an identification -- but the general description does not validate the identification. If the initial description was "medium height, medium build, dark clothing" -- this describes thousands of people and does not corroborate the identification.

**Factor 4: The Level of Certainty Demonstrated by the Witness at the Confrontation**

| Sub-Factor | Assessment | Supporting Science |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| What did the witness say at the time of identification? | [Evaluate verbatim] | Verbatim language is critical |
| Was the statement confident or tentative? | [Evaluate] | Initial tentative IDs that later become "certain" are a red flag |
| Was the confidence statement recorded immediately? | [Evaluate] | Delayed recording allows feedback contamination |
| Was any feedback given before the confidence was recorded? | [Evaluate] | Wells & Bradfield (1998) -- feedback inflates confidence |
| Has the witness's expressed confidence increased over time? | [Evaluate] | Confidence inflation is a hallmark of unreliable identification |

> **NAS Report (2014) at 4:** "The confidence an eyewitness expresses in an identification, at least at the time it is made, is informative. But confidence can be inflated by factors that have nothing to do with accuracy, such as post-identification feedback. The only confidence assessment that is probative is the one made at the time of the initial identification, before any confirming feedback."

> **Critical defense point -- the confidence-accuracy relationship:** Courts have traditionally treated witness confidence as a strong indicator of accuracy. The science tells a more nuanced story. Under pristine conditions (double-blind, unbiased instructions, no feedback), initial confidence and accuracy are moderately correlated. Under imperfect conditions (which describe most real-world identifications), the confidence-accuracy relationship deteriorates significantly. By the time a witness testifies at trial -- months or years after the identification, after receiving confirming feedback, after repeated exposure to the defendant -- the confidence expressed on the witness stand has no reliable relationship to accuracy.

**Factor 5: The Length of Time Between the Crime and the Confrontation**

| Sub-Factor | Assessment | Supporting Science |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| How much time elapsed? | [Document precisely] | Memory decay follows Ebbinghaus curve |
| What happened during the interval? | [Evaluate] | Post-event information, co-witness discussion, media exposure |
| Were there intermediate identification procedures? | [Evaluate] | Each intermediate procedure contaminates the memory |

### F.2 -- Scientific Critique of the Manson Framework

The NAS Report (2014) and the majority of eyewitness researchers have criticized the Manson/Brathwaite framework as scientifically outdated:

1. **The framework treats reliability factors as independent.** They are not. A witness who had a poor opportunity to view, was under stress, and received confirming feedback has a reliability problem that compounds -- the factors do not merely add; they multiply the risk.

2. **The framework permits a suggestive procedure to be "saved" by reliability factors.** But the science demonstrates that suggestiveness corrupts the very factors the court uses to assess reliability -- particularly confidence. The test is internally contradictory: the court uses confidence to assess whether the identification is reliable enough to admit despite suggestiveness, but the suggestiveness itself inflated the confidence.

3. **The framework was decided before the modern science.** Manson (1977) predates the vast majority of eyewitness research. The NAS Report (2014) specifically recommended replacing the Manson framework with a more scientifically grounded approach.

4. **Henderson v. New Jersey (2011)** adopted a reformed framework recognizing system and estimator variables. While Henderson is not binding in Louisiana, it represents the direction of the science and provides persuasive authority.

> **Advocacy point:** When applying the Manson factors, argue that the court should consider the modern science underlying each factor, not merely the factor labels. The Supreme Court in Manson did not have the benefit of 40+ years of controlled research on eyewitness identification. Present the science alongside the legal framework.
