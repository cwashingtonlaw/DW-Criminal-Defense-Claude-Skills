# Analyst Dossier — Compilation Template & Cautionary Precedents

This reference is loaded by `dw-crime-lab-auditor-crim` Module C. It provides a template for compiling the professional record of every testifying lab analyst, the discovery demand checklist for obtaining the underlying records, and the cautionary precedents the defense bar must know.

The analyst dossier is the centerpiece of any contested lab case. The State frames the analyst as a neutral scientist; the defense's job is to put the analyst into the same evidentiary frame as any other witness — qualifications subject to inspection, prior testimony available for impeachment, error history disclosed under *Brady/Giglio*.

---

## 1. Dossier Template

Compile one dossier per testifying analyst. Each section below corresponds to a specific discovery item.

### A. Identification

- Full legal name (and any prior names)
- Current employer / lab / section
- Job title (criminalist, forensic chemist, forensic toxicologist, technical lead, supervisor)
- Date hired into current position
- Date hired into the lab
- Prior employment (relevant — prior labs, prior agencies)

### B. Education

- Degrees (BA/BS, MS, PhD) with institution, major, and date
- Coursework relevant to forensic chemistry / toxicology (analytical chemistry, instrumental analysis, biochemistry, pharmacology)
- Continuing education and training courses (lab-internal, vendor training, professional society)
- **Audit point:** does the analyst's degree and coursework support the specific methodology testified to? A B.S. in biology is sufficient for some forensic-chemistry roles but not for others.

### C. Certifications

The two most relevant professional certifications:

- **ABC** — American Board of Criminalistics (Drug Analysis specialty; Molecular Biology specialty; etc.)
- **ABFT** — American Board of Forensic Toxicology (Forensic Toxicology Specialist; Forensic Toxicology Diplomate)

Other relevant:
- **DABCC-TC** — Diplomate of the American Board of Clinical Chemistry, Toxicological Chemistry
- **AAFS** Fellow / Member status (not a certification per se but a credential)
- Vendor-specific certifications (e.g., Agilent GC/MS operator certification)

**Audit point:** is the analyst certified in the specialty relevant to the case? A drug-analyst (ABC) is not a toxicologist (ABFT) and should not be testifying to blood-tox interpretation outside the drug-ID context.

### D. Lab Role and Tenure

- Section assignment (drug chemistry, toxicology, trace, firearms, DNA — DNA out of scope here)
- Authorization scope — what methods is the analyst authorized to perform unsupervised?
- Tenure in the section
- Supervisory role (analyst, technical lead, section supervisor, lab director)
- Promotion history

### E. Proficiency Test History

Proficiency tests verify ongoing competency. Two kinds:

1. **Internal proficiency tests** — administered by the lab's quality manager, typically annually, using known samples
2. **External proficiency tests** — administered by an outside provider (most commonly **Collaborative Testing Services (CTS)** — verify current)

**Required disclosure points:**
- All proficiency tests for the analyst over the past 3-5 years (best practice — past 5)
- Pass / fail outcome for each
- If failed: the corrective action taken, the retest result, and whether re-authorization to perform the method was granted
- The samples used and the analyte concentrations
- The analyst's reported result vs. the expected result

**Critical finding triggers:**
- Any failed proficiency test in the relevant methodology — **CRITICAL**
- Pattern of marginal passes (results at the edge of acceptance limits) — **SIGNIFICANT**
- Refusal or failure to produce the proficiency record — first audit finding (Brady demand)

### F. Prior Testimony

- List of cases in which the analyst has testified (court, docket, judge, year)
- Any Daubert / Foret hearing transcripts available
- **Successful Daubert challenges or exclusions** — these are *Giglio* material
- **Prior impeachment** — was the analyst impeached on a specific point in a prior case?
- **Inconsistent prior testimony** — has the analyst testified one way in case X and a different way in case Y on the same methodology?

**Discovery vehicles:**
- Subpoena to the lab for the analyst's testimony log
- *Giglio* request to the State
- LexisNexis / Westlaw search by analyst name
- Defense-bar listserv inquiries

### G. Error Logs / Corrective Actions

Every accredited lab maintains an internal nonconformance / corrective-action system. Entries naming the analyst or affecting methods the analyst performs are *Brady/Giglio* material.

- Mistakes (sample mix-up, contamination, instrumental error attributed to operator)
- Corrective actions imposed (retraining, re-authorization, supervisor review of next N cases)
- Root-cause analyses where the analyst was identified

**Discovery vehicle:** specific Brady/Giglio demand for the analyst's error-log entries and any internal-affairs / quality-investigation file.

### H. Misconduct / Discipline History

- Formal discipline (suspension, demotion, termination)
- Internal investigations (whether resolved adverse or favorable to the analyst)
- Audit findings naming the analyst
- Civil suits or licensure proceedings against the analyst
- Criminal proceedings against the analyst

### I. Brady Disclosures by the Lab

- Has the lab made any blanket *Brady* disclosure regarding this analyst?
- Is the analyst on a known "do not call" list (e.g., a District Attorney's Office *Giglio* list)?
- Has the lab issued any letter to defense counsel disclosing analyst-related issues in cases handled?

## 2. Cautionary Precedents

Every defense lawyer auditing a crime lab must know these cases. They establish what the worst-case crime-lab misconduct looks like, what defense lawyers were unable to learn at the time, and what *Brady/Giglio* now requires.

### Annie Dookhan — Massachusetts (2003-2012)

**The misconduct:** Annie Dookhan was a chemist at the William A. Hinton State Laboratory Institute in Jamaica Plain, Massachusetts. Over nine years she handled an estimated **24,000+ drug cases**. She systematically falsified results — "dry-labbing" (reporting results without performing the test), forging colleagues' initials on documents, contaminating samples to produce positive results in cases where her initial test was negative, and lying about credentials (she falsely claimed an MS in chemistry).

**How it was discovered:** A colleague noticed Dookhan signing out evidence she had not been authorized to test. An internal investigation expanded to a criminal investigation.

**The fallout:** Dookhan pleaded guilty in 2013 to obstruction of justice, perjury, and evidence tampering and was sentenced to 3-5 years in prison (paroled 2016). The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court vacated convictions in cases she had touched in **Bridgeman v. District Attorney** and successor cases — ultimately tens of thousands of convictions dismissed. Massachusetts continues to litigate Dookhan-tainted cases more than a decade later.

**Defense lesson:** When an analyst handles unusually high case volumes, that is an indicator — Dookhan's productivity should have been impossible and was a documented red flag. The defense bar must demand productivity statistics and Brady/Giglio material on every analyst, not just those already exposed.

### Sonja Farak — Massachusetts (2003-2013)

**The misconduct:** Sonja Farak, also a Massachusetts state chemist (Amherst Drug Lab), was caught in 2013 in possession of methamphetamine and cocaine while at work. Investigation revealed she had been **using drugs from the lab's evidence inventory and standards** for years — including during workdays — while performing identifications and certifying results.

**How it was discovered:** A coworker found a substance in her workstation; lab management called State Police.

**The fallout:** Farak pleaded guilty in 2014 to evidence tampering and drug-possession charges. The Attorney General's office initially **concealed the full scope** of her misconduct, leading to a 2017 Massachusetts SJC finding of prosecutorial misconduct (*Committee for Public Counsel Services v. Attorney General*, 480 Mass. 700 (2018)). Thousands of convictions were vacated.

**Defense lesson:** Even when misconduct is discovered, the State may attempt to limit disclosure to the minimum legally required. Brady-Giglio enforcement requires aggressive demands and judicial supervision. The defense cannot accept the State's word that the misconduct was "limited" — independent investigation is required.

### Houston Crime Lab — Multiple Decades of Misconduct

**The misconduct:** The Houston Police Department Crime Lab was repeatedly cited for systemic failures across multiple disciplines from the 1990s through the 2000s. The **DNA section was closed in 2002** after a Texas A&M audit identified contamination, unqualified analysts, and fabricated documentation. Subsequent reviews found problems in serology, toxicology, drug analysis, and trace evidence.

**The fallout:** Hundreds of convictions reviewed; multiple exonerations; criminal investigations into specific analysts; the lab eventually transitioned to the Houston Forensic Science Center (HFSC), an independent local government corporation.

**Defense lesson:** Lab misconduct can be **structural** — caused by poor management, inadequate funding, lack of accreditation, and the absence of external oversight — not merely individual rogue actors. When defense lawyers identify a structurally compromised lab, every case from that lab during the period of compromise is potentially affected.

### Detroit Crime Lab — Closed 2008

**The misconduct:** A 2008 Michigan State Police audit of the Detroit Police Department Crime Lab's firearms unit found a **10% error rate** in case reviews — work product so unreliable that the Wayne County Prosecutor stopped accepting the lab's results. The lab was closed.

**The fallout:** Hundreds of cases reopened; a significant impact on Wayne County prosecutions; ongoing post-conviction litigation.

**Defense lesson:** External audits are the most reliable source of structural information about a lab's reliability. Demand the audit reports, and pay attention to acceptance-of-results decisions by local prosecutor's offices — they signal what insiders already know.

### North Carolina SBI Lab — Major Reform After Revealed Misconduct

**The misconduct:** A 2010 investigation commissioned by the Attorney General (the **Swecker Report**) found that the State Bureau of Investigation's serology unit had systematically **omitted exculpatory results** from lab reports — reporting confirmatory positives but suppressing inconclusive or negative confirmatory tests on the same samples. The practice affected at least 230 cases over 16 years.

**The fallout:** Major statewide reforms; legislative oversight; multiple convictions vacated; criminal and civil litigation against specific analysts.

**Defense lesson:** "Negative or inconclusive results are part of the report" is a discovery demand that should be made in every case. The omission of unfavorable results is *Brady* misconduct even when the favorable results are accurately reported.

### Pattern recognition

Across Dookhan, Farak, Houston, Detroit, and NC SBI, defense lawyers can identify common red flags:

1. **High case volume** per analyst — productivity beyond normal benchmarks
2. **Single analyst handles all stages** without independent review or sign-off
3. **Absence of accreditation** or accreditation under a lenient standard
4. **Limited external audit** or repeated negative external audits
5. **Internal opacity** — corrective actions, error logs, and proficiency records not produced in discovery
6. **Limited prior Daubert challenges** in the jurisdiction — suggesting the defense bar has not yet stress-tested the lab

When two or more of these indicators are present, escalate the audit. Recommend a defense lab-systems / quality auditor and consider broader Brady litigation.

## 3. Discovery Demand Checklist — Analyst Dossier

Use this as the body of a Brady/Giglio motion or a discovery letter to the State.

### Identification & Education
- [ ] Current and prior CVs / résumés
- [ ] Educational transcripts or degree verification
- [ ] Course descriptions for relevant coursework
- [ ] Continuing-education records for the past 5 years

### Certifications
- [ ] ABC, ABFT, or other professional certifications, with dates and any lapses
- [ ] Vendor / instrument-specific certifications

### Lab Role
- [ ] Authorization records — what methods is the analyst authorized to perform?
- [ ] Date of authorization for the specific method used in this case
- [ ] Supervisor reviews of the analyst's casework

### Proficiency Tests
- [ ] All proficiency tests (internal and external) for the past 5 years
- [ ] Results, including any failures
- [ ] Corrective actions taken on failures
- [ ] Re-authorization records following any failure

### Prior Testimony
- [ ] Testimony log maintained by the lab
- [ ] Transcripts of Daubert / Foret hearings involving this analyst
- [ ] Any documented exclusions or limitations placed on the analyst's testimony in prior cases
- [ ] Cases in which the analyst was impeached on a specific point relevant here

### Error Logs / Corrective Actions
- [ ] Nonconformance reports involving the analyst (past 5 years)
- [ ] Corrective-action plans
- [ ] Root-cause analyses
- [ ] Internal investigations

### Discipline / Misconduct
- [ ] Disciplinary actions by the lab, the agency, or any licensing body
- [ ] Internal-affairs files
- [ ] Civil or criminal proceedings against the analyst
- [ ] Any DA's office *Giglio* list inclusion

### Lab-Wide Brady / Giglio
- [ ] Audit reports from ANAB or the lab's accrediting body for the past 5 years
- [ ] Corrective-action plans on lab-wide findings
- [ ] Any blanket Brady disclosures issued by the lab
- [ ] *Brady*-list inclusion of the analyst or the lab section

### When the State Refuses

If the State refuses or returns nothing, the audit's **FIRST FINDING** is the refusal — escalate to a motion to compel, with citation to *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963); *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150 (1972); *Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419 (1995); and La. C.Cr.P. Art. 718-722. Treat refusal as itself the most important Brady issue in the case.

## Module C Short Version (moved from SKILL.md)

Short version: compile a full dossier on the testifying analyst — CV, certifications (ABC, ABFT), tenure, proficiency-test history (pass/fail), prior testimony record (Daubert challenges, exclusions), error-log entries naming the analyst, and discipline history. Cross-reference against the cautionary precedents (Dookhan, Farak, Houston, Detroit, NC SBI) — when systemic misconduct has occurred at a lab, the defense is entitled to it under Brady regardless of whether *this* analyst was directly implicated.
