# Closing Argument — Louisiana Guardrails

Detailed rules for Louisiana criminal closing arguments. This file is the enforcement checklist for `dw-trial-narrative-builder-crim` STEP 4. Run every draft closing through this checklist before delivering to the attorney.

Closing argument is the defense's only chance to argue the meaning of the evidence — what it shows, what it lacks, what conclusions the jury must draw from it. Within the bounds of Art. 774, closing is the moment to harvest the themes planted in opening, walk the jury through the reasonable-doubt instruction, and inoculate against the State's rebuttal.

---

## Foundational Louisiana Authority

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 774 — Scope of Closing

Art. 774 confines closing argument to:
1. The evidence admitted at trial
2. The lack of evidence at trial
3. Conclusions of fact that may be drawn from the evidence
4. The law applicable to the case (as stated in the jury instructions)

Anything outside these four categories is improper. The most common Art. 774 violations:
- Reference to facts not in evidence (witnesses not called, exhibits not offered, statements never made)
- Misstatement of law (especially the reasonable-doubt standard, the burden of proof, the presumption of innocence)
- Personal opinion and vouching
- Appeals to passion or prejudice
- Comment on defendant's silence (Griffin/Doyle — distinct from Art. 774 but cited contemporaneously)

**Enforcement:** Every assertion in the closing must be traceable to (a) admitted evidence, (b) the absence of evidence, (c) an inference reasonably drawn from admitted evidence, or (d) the court's instructions. If an assertion does not fall in one of these four buckets, strike it.

### State Rebuttal Scope

The defense closing is followed by the State's rebuttal. Rebuttal is also bounded by Art. 774, AND is further bounded by the scope of the defense closing: the State may rebut only what the defense argued, not introduce new affirmative argument. *State v. Sayles*, 395 So.2d 695 (La. 1981). When the State's rebuttal exceeds scope, the defense must object contemporaneously to preserve the issue (La. C.Cr.P. Art. 841). See `rebuttal-anticipation.md` for the scope-of-rebuttal objection template.

## The Reasonable Doubt Instruction — Mirror It Verbatim

### Authority

- *Cage v. Louisiana*, 498 U.S. 39 (1990) — original SCOTUS reversal; Louisiana's prior reasonable-doubt formulation violated the Due Process Clause because the phrases "substantial doubt," "grave uncertainty," and "actual substantial doubt" allowed conviction on a lower standard than due process requires.
- *State v. Cage*, 583 So.2d 1125 (La. 1991) — on remand. Louisiana redefined the reasonable-doubt instruction with three required phrases that satisfy due process: (1) "grave uncertainty," (2) "real, tangible, substantial basis," and (3) "such a doubt as would give rise to a grave uncertainty / such a doubt as a reasonable person would have when acting in a matter of the gravest concern in his own affairs."
- *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970) — due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of a charged offense.
- *Victor v. Nebraska*, 511 U.S. 1 (1994) — the Constitution does not require any particular form of words; the instruction must convey the concept correctly. The Cage formulation satisfies Victor.
- *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993) — a constitutionally deficient reasonable-doubt instruction is structural error, not subject to harmless-error review.

### Louisiana's Reasonable Doubt Redefinition History

The trajectory: prior to *Cage v. Louisiana* (1990), Louisiana used a reasonable-doubt instruction that the U.S. Supreme Court found unconstitutional. The Louisiana Supreme Court rewrote the instruction in *State v. Cage* on remand. The current Cage-compliant formulation is the only formulation that survives constitutional review.

### What the Defense Closing Must Do

The defense closing must mirror the Cage-compliant jury-instruction language verbatim. The jury will hear the language from the judge's charge; if the defense closing uses the same phrasing, the jury hears the reasonable-doubt standard three times — once in the defense closing, once in the judge's charge, and once when the jury deliberates. Mirroring locks the instruction into the jury's memory.

**Required phrases (use verbatim):**
- "grave uncertainty"
- "real, tangible, substantial basis"
- "such a doubt as a reasonable person would have when acting in a matter of the gravest concern in his own affairs"

**Avoid (these phrases are either reversible-error or weaken the defense):**
- "Beyond all doubt" — wrong standard; the State only has to prove beyond REASONABLE doubt
- "Mathematical certainty" — wrong standard
- "Possible doubt" / "any doubt" — the State will pounce; the jury instruction explicitly excludes mere possible doubt
- "Articulated reason for doubt" — a juror need not be able to articulate why they have doubt; the doubt is enough
- Any rephrasing of the Cage-required phrases — use the words from the instruction

### What the State Will Try In Rebuttal

The State often tries to minimize reasonable doubt: "Reasonable doubt is not any doubt; it's a real doubt; use your common sense; you would not refuse to act in your own affairs because of every theoretical possibility." Some of this is permitted argument; some of it crosses into misstating the law. The defense closing should pre-rebut this by reciting the instruction verbatim and saying *"That's the standard the judge will give you. Hold the State to it."* See `rebuttal-anticipation.md`.

## The Burden — Never Shifts

### Authority

- *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)
- *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993)
- *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979) — mandatory presumptions that shift the burden on an element of the offense violate due process
- *Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307 (1985) — jury instructions that even arguably shift the burden on an element violate due process

### What the Defense Closing Must Say

The burden remains with the State throughout the trial. The defense need not prove anything. The defendant need not testify. The jury need not believe the defense theory. The jury need only have a reasonable doubt about whether the State has proven every element of every charge beyond a reasonable doubt.

This must be said multiple times in different words throughout the closing. The "burden hammer" section of the closing is its rhetorical core.

### What the Defense Closing Must Not Say (Sandstrom Concerns)

The defense closing must not invite the jury to apply a presumption against the defendant. Some examples of language to avoid:
- "If the defendant did X, then he must have intended Y" — invites a mandatory presumption
- "You can assume that..." (about a fact the State has not proved) — invites a presumption
- Anything that suggests the jury can shortcut an element by inference rather than by proof

The defense closing is also free to attack Sandstrom violations in the State's closing — if the prosecutor invites a presumption, the defense objects and, on appeal, raises the issue.

## Constitutional Floor — Same as Opening

The constitutional limits on opening apply also to closing, with the difference that argument (about evidence) is permitted in closing but not in opening:

### 1. No Comment on Defendant's Silence

**Authority:** *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965); *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976).

The defense closing cannot reference the defendant's failure to testify, the defendant's failure to give a statement to police, the defendant's invocation of Miranda, or any other invocation of silence. Even framing it positively ("Mr. Cole had the right to remain silent and exercised it") is dangerous because it draws attention to the silence in a way the constitutional rule was designed to prevent.

If the State references the defendant's silence (or the defendant's "failure to explain"), the defense objects under Griffin/Doyle and requests a curative instruction. *State v. Smith*, 327 So.2d 355 (La. 1976); *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967) (harmless error analysis for constitutional comment on silence).

### 2. No Personal Vouching

**Authority:** La. Rules of Prof. Conduct 3.4(e).

"I believe," "I know," "in my opinion," "trust me" — all prohibited. Counsel may argue the evidence; counsel may not inject personal belief. The line: "The evidence shows X" is permitted; "I believe X" is not.

### 3. No Name-Calling

The defense closing cannot characterize the State, the prosecutor, or any State witness in personally derogatory terms. "Detective Smith lied" is permitted IF supported by evidence (his testimony contradicts his report); "Detective Smith is a liar" without evidentiary support crosses the line. Courts vary on how strictly this is enforced, but the safer practice is to argue the conduct, not the character.

### 4. No Appeals to Passion or Prejudice (Golden Rule)

**Authority:** Louisiana practice; widely recognized doctrine.

The defense closing cannot ask the jury to put themselves in the defendant's shoes, in the victim's shoes, or in any party's shoes. Defense closings sometimes try a softer variant ("any of us, in this situation, might have done the same...") — this is still a Golden Rule violation and is properly objected to.

Permitted variant: describe the circumstances objectively and let the jury draw their own conclusions. "It was 2:00 in the morning. The street was dark. Three men were approaching. Mr. Cole could not see their faces clearly. That is the evidence." The jury will infer the human response; the defense does not need to ask them to inhabit it.

### 5. No Reference to Facts Not in Evidence

Closing argument is bounded by what was admitted at trial. If a fact was excluded by motion in limine, ruled hearsay, or simply never offered, it cannot appear in closing. The State will object, the court will sustain, and the curative instruction will damage the defense.

**Enforcement:** Cross-check every factual reference in the draft closing against the actual trial record. Any reference to a fact not admitted must be struck.

### 6. No Misstatement of Law

Closing argument can argue the law, but only as stated in the jury instructions. The defense cannot tell the jury that reasonable doubt means something different from what the judge will tell them. The defense cannot tell the jury that the State has a higher or lower burden than the instructions state. The defense cannot characterize an affirmative-defense burden incorrectly.

**The closing must mirror the jury instructions.** Pull the instruction text from `dw-jury-instructions-builder-crim` and quote it directly.

## Reference to Inadmissible Evidence — Mid-Trial Issues That Become Closing Problems

Sometimes during trial, evidence is admitted that the defense believes was wrongly admitted (e.g., 404(B) evidence, prior bad acts, witness hearsay). The defense cannot ignore admitted evidence in closing — but the defense can argue its limitations. Permitted closing language for problematic admitted evidence:
- "You heard the testimony about [prior incident]. The judge will instruct you that you may consider that testimony only for the limited purpose of [purpose]. You may not use it to conclude that Mr. Cole is a bad person or that he has a disposition to commit crimes." (Mirroring the La. C.E. Art. 404(B) limiting instruction.)
- "You heard [witness] testify that [hearsay-adjacent statement]. Notice what was missing: the actual person who made that statement did not testify. You did not hear from [original speaker]."

This is permitted closing argument and is properly framed as an inference from the LACK of evidence (Art. 774(2)).

## Berger v. United States — Defense Use Against Prosecutorial Misconduct

### Authority

*Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78 (1935) — a prosecutor's improper closing argument can warrant reversal. The Berger framework: the prosecutor is the representative of a sovereign and has a duty to refrain from improper methods calculated to produce a wrongful conviction.

### Defense Use

When the State commits closing-argument misconduct (vouching, Golden Rule, comment on silence, misstatement of law, appeals to passion), the defense:
1. Objects contemporaneously (Art. 841)
2. Requests a curative instruction
3. Moves for a mistrial if the misconduct is severe
4. Preserves the issue for appellate review under Berger

The defense closing itself does not invoke Berger affirmatively, but the rebuttal-anticipation memo (`rebuttal-anticipation.md`) should flag predicted State misconduct points so the attorney is ready to object during rebuttal.

## Preserving Error in Argument for Appeal

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 841 — Contemporaneous Objection

Errors in closing argument must be objected to at the time, on the record, with specific grounds. Failure to object waives the issue for appeal. The defense must:
1. Stand and say "Objection — [specific ground: comment on silence / misstatement of law / facts not in evidence / improper vouching / Golden Rule / scope of rebuttal]"
2. Wait for the court's ruling
3. If overruled and the misconduct is severe, request a curative instruction or move for mistrial
4. Note the ruling in the record so the appellate court can review

### Integration with dw-appellate-error-monitor-crim

Every closing-argument error preserved should be logged with `dw-appellate-error-monitor-crim` for inclusion in the post-trial error preservation record. The trial narrative builder's Rebuttal Anticipation Memo identifies predicted errors; `dw-appellate-error-monitor-crim` tracks which predictions came true and which were preserved.

## The Draft-Time Checklist (Mandatory Before Delivery)

Run every draft closing through this checklist. Mark each item PASS or FAIL. Do not deliver a draft with any FAIL items.

- [ ] Every factual assertion is traceable to admitted trial evidence
- [ ] No reference to facts not in evidence
- [ ] No reference to motion-in-limine-excluded evidence
- [ ] No comment, direct or indirect, on defendant's pretrial silence
- [ ] No comment on defendant's failure to testify
- [ ] Reasonable-doubt language mirrors the Cage-compliant jury instruction verbatim (three required phrases present)
- [ ] No "beyond all doubt" or "mathematical certainty" framing
- [ ] No personal vouching ("I believe," "I know," etc.)
- [ ] No Sandstrom-style presumption-shifting language
- [ ] No Golden Rule invitation
- [ ] No name-calling unsupported by evidence
- [ ] Affirmative-defense burden (if any) stated correctly per jury instruction
- [ ] Theme callbacks use verbatim opening-statement phrasing
- [ ] Every promise made in opening has been either delivered or inoculated
- [ ] Pre-rebuttal lines for predicted State rebuttal points are woven in
- [ ] Verdict-form walk-through is included
- [ ] Time-limit compliant (rule of thumb: 150 words per spoken minute)
- [ ] Ends on the registered theme line — same words as opening

---

## When a Draft Fails the Checklist

If any item fails, the skill rewrites the offending passage and re-runs the checklist. The skill does not deliver a draft with known violations. If the attorney overrides, document the override in draft notes and flag for attorney signature.
