# Opening Statement — Louisiana Guardrails

Detailed rules for Louisiana criminal opening statements. This file is the enforcement checklist for `dw-trial-narrative-builder-crim` STEP 3. Run every draft opening through this checklist before delivering to the attorney. A draft that violates any item must be fixed, not delivered.

---

## Foundational Louisiana Authority

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 765 — Opening Statement Is Permitted But Bounded

Louisiana permits the defense to make an opening statement after the State's opening, or to reserve opening until the start of the defense case. The opening is a **preview of what the evidence will show**. It is not an argument about what the evidence means. Louisiana courts will sustain "argument in opening" objections, and a habitual violator risks not only an objection sustained in front of the jury but a curative instruction that undercuts the defense.

**Practical rule:** Anything that begins with "the evidence will show" is preview. Anything that begins with "this proves" or "this means" or "you can see that" is argument — save it for closing.

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 774 — Scope of Argument

Art. 774 governs the scope of argument and applies primarily to closing, but courts apply its principles to opening as well: argument must be confined to the evidence admitted, the lack of evidence, conclusions of fact that may be drawn from the evidence, and the law applicable to the case. In opening, the relevant constraint is the first prong: the opening previews what the evidence WILL be admitted. References to evidence that will not be admitted (excluded by motion in limine, inadmissible hearsay, etc.) are improper.

## The Constitutional Floor — Things That Never Appear in Opening

### 1. No Comment on Defendant's Silence

**Authority:** *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965); *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976).

The defense cannot reference the defendant's pretrial silence in a way that asks the jury to draw any inference from it. (The State certainly cannot — but the defense must also be careful not to invite the issue.) More commonly, this comes up when the defense wants to preview the defendant's account of what happened. **If the defendant will not testify, the defense opening cannot promise the defendant's account from the defendant's mouth.** The opening must promise the defense story will come from "the evidence" — exhibits, cross-examination, defense witnesses — not from the defendant directly.

If the defendant WILL testify, the opening may preview the defendant's testimony, but should do so cautiously and only after the attorney has confirmed the defendant will in fact testify. **Never promise the defendant's testimony and then fail to call the defendant.** This is a State closing-argument weapon: "Defense counsel promised you would hear from Mr. Cole. Where is Mr. Cole?" The Griffin/Doyle line protects against State comment on silence — it does not protect the defense from its own broken promises.

**Enforcement:** Strike any sentence in opening that references whether the defendant did or did not speak to police, did or did not give a statement, or did or did not testify before the grand jury, unless that reference is part of an admissible suppression-related point and has been pre-cleared with the court.

### 2. No Shifting of Burden of Proof

**Authority:** *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993).

The defense opening cannot say "we will show," "we will prove," or "we will demonstrate" in a way that suggests the defense has a burden to prove anything. The burden is on the State throughout. The defense's job in opening is to preview the evidence the jury will see — not to take on a burden.

**Safer language:** "You will hear evidence that..." / "The evidence will show that..." / "When the police body-cam plays, you will see..." — these phrasings do not suggest a defense burden. They describe what the jury will receive.

**Avoid:** "We will prove our client is innocent." / "The defense will demonstrate that..." — both suggest a defense burden the defense does not have.

### 3. No Personal Vouching by Counsel

**Authority:** La. Rules of Prof. Conduct 3.4(e).

Phrases that violate the vouching rule:
- "I believe..."
- "I know this man is innocent."
- "In my opinion..."
- "I am convinced that..."
- "Trust me, the evidence will show..."

Counsel may not interject personal opinion or personal belief about the case. Counsel may say "the evidence will show" — that is a prediction about evidence, not a personal vouching. The distinction matters and Louisiana courts (and bar disciplinary panels) take it seriously.

**Enforcement:** Strike every "I believe," "I know," "in my opinion," "trust me," and equivalent first-person belief assertion in the draft opening. Replace with "the evidence will show," "you will hear," or "you will see."

### 4. No Argument About What the Evidence MEANS

**Authority:** La. C.Cr.P. Art. 765 (implicit); Louisiana practice.

The line between preview and argument is the line between WHAT the evidence is and WHAT IT MEANS. Examples:

| Preview (permitted in opening) | Argument (closing only) |
|---|---|
| "You will see Officer Smith's body-cam video. The video shows him approaching Mr. Cole at 9:14 PM." | "Officer Smith's body-cam video proves Mr. Cole was not the aggressor." |
| "You will hear Detective Jones testify that the lab report came back two months after the arrest." | "The two-month delay shows the police were not being thorough — they were building a case." |
| "Three witnesses will testify that Mr. Cole was at his mother's house at the time of the shooting." | "These three witnesses, taken together, give Mr. Cole a complete alibi." |

The preview describes the evidence the jury will receive. The argument tells the jury what to conclude. Save the conclusion for closing.

**Enforcement:** For every sentence in the draft opening, ask: "Does this sentence tell the jury what they will SEE, or does it tell them what to THINK about what they will see?" If the latter, rewrite or strike.

### 5. No Reference to Inadmissible Evidence

If the court has granted a motion in limine excluding certain evidence, that evidence cannot be referenced in opening. The same applies to evidence the defense knows will not survive an evidentiary objection (hearsay without exception, prior bad acts without 404(B) notice, etc.).

**Enforcement:** Cross-check every factual reference in the draft opening against the motion-in-limine rulings in the case file. Any reference to excluded evidence must be struck.

### 6. No Golden Rule Violation

**Authority:** Louisiana practice; widely recognized prosecutorial-misconduct doctrine.

The "Golden Rule" prohibits asking jurors to put themselves in the place of a party — the defendant, the victim, the witness. "Imagine if it were your son who was arrested." "Put yourself in Mr. Cole's shoes." "What would you have done?" All improper.

The defense is sometimes tempted to ask the jury to imagine the defendant's circumstances ("imagine you were Mr. Cole, late at night, in a dark alley, with three men approaching..."). This is a Golden Rule violation regardless of which side commits it. The defense should describe Mr. Cole's circumstances objectively, not ask the jury to inhabit them.

**Enforcement:** Strike any sentence that begins with "Imagine if..." "Put yourself in..." "What would you have done if..." or any equivalent invitation for the juror to substitute their own perspective for that of a party.

### 7. Estelle v. Williams — Restraint and Clothing Considerations

**Authority:** *Estelle v. Williams*, 425 U.S. 501 (1976).

*Estelle v. Williams* prohibits the State from compelling the defendant to appear before the jury in identifiable prison clothing or visible restraints. The defense cannot reference the defendant's clothing, appearance, or restraints in opening — drawing attention to them undercuts the presumption of innocence the defendant is constitutionally entitled to.

**Enforcement:** Strike any sentence in opening that comments on the defendant's appearance, clothing, demeanor, or physical condition. The opening should treat the defendant as a person with a presumption of innocence, not as a defendant on display.

### 8. Promising Evidence the Defense Cannot Deliver

**Authority:** Louisiana practice; effective-assistance jurisprudence.

The single most damaging unforced error in a defense opening is promising the jury evidence that does not come in. The State's closing weapon: "Defense counsel told you at the start of this trial that you would see X. Did you see X? You did not. Why? Because there is no X." This destroys defense credibility for the duration of the trial.

**Mitigation:**
- Every exhibit foreshadowed in opening must be on the defense exhibit list and confirmed admissible.
- Every witness referenced in opening must be on the defense witness list and confirmed available.
- If an exhibit's admissibility is contingent on a foundation that has not yet been laid, mark the opening line `[CONTINGENT — confirm before delivery]` and resolve before opening day.

**Enforcement:** Run the promise/deliver audit at draft time AND at MODE B (closing). The closing must inoculate against any promise that didn't deliver.

## Objection Triggers — Anticipate and Avoid

Common State objections during defense opening, and how to draft to avoid them:

| State objection | Trigger language | Safer draft language |
|---|---|---|
| "Argument" | "This proves..." "The truth is..." "What really happened..." | "The evidence will show..." "You will hear testimony..." "When you see Exhibit D-7..." |
| "Facts not in evidence" | Reference to documents/witnesses not on the case list | Reference only confirmed exhibits and witnesses |
| "Improper vouching" | "I believe," "I know," "Trust me" | "The evidence," "You will see," "You will hear" |
| "Reference to inadmissible evidence" | Reference to motion-in-limine excluded items | Cross-check exclusion list before drafting |
| "Burden shifting" | "We will prove" / "We will show our client is innocent" | "The State must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt; the evidence will fall short" |
| "Golden Rule" | "Imagine if..." / "Put yourself in..." | Objective description without inviting juror substitution |
| "Comment on silence" | Reference to defendant's silence to police, grand jury, prior proceeding | Avoid entirely unless pre-cleared |

## The Draft-Time Checklist (Mandatory Before Delivery)

Run every draft opening through this checklist. Mark each item PASS or FAIL. Do not deliver a draft with any FAIL items.

- [ ] No comment, direct or indirect, on defendant's pretrial silence
- [ ] No "I believe," "I know," "I am convinced," or other personal vouching
- [ ] No "we will prove" language suggesting a defense burden
- [ ] No conclusory argument — only previews of what evidence will show
- [ ] No reference to motion-in-limine-excluded evidence
- [ ] No Golden Rule invitation
- [ ] No reference to defendant's clothing, restraints, or appearance
- [ ] Every exhibit foreshadowed is on the exhibit list and admissible
- [ ] Every witness referenced is on the witness list and available
- [ ] No reference to facts not expected to be in evidence
- [ ] Reasonable-doubt anchoring language mirrors the Cage-compliant jury instruction
- [ ] No Sandstrom-style presumption language
- [ ] Time-limit compliant (rule of thumb: 150 words per spoken minute)
- [ ] Themes 1-N registered with the verbatim phrasing that will be repeated in mid-trial and closing
- [ ] Ends on a memorable theme line or one-sentence inoculation

---

## When a Draft Fails the Checklist

If any item fails, the skill rewrites the offending passage and re-runs the checklist. The skill does not deliver a draft with known violations. If the attorney overrides the skill's correction and insists on language that violates a guardrail, the skill documents the override in the opening's draft notes (header comment) and flags it for the attorney's signature so the override is on the record.
