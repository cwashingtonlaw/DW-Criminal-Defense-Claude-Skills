## Part I — Louisiana Code of Evidence

### La. C.E. Art. 611 — Mode and order of interrogation and presentation

The governing rule on direct-exam form.

**Art. 611(A) — Control by the court:** The court controls the mode and order of examining witnesses to (1) make interrogation effective for the ascertainment of truth, (2) avoid needless consumption of time, and (3) protect witnesses from harassment.

**Art. 611(B) — Scope of cross-examination:** A witness may be cross-examined on any matter relevant to any issue in the case, including credibility. (Louisiana follows the "wide-open" or "wide-latitude" cross rule — broader than the federal "scope of direct" rule under F.R.E. 611(b).)

> **Defense implication:** When the defense calls a witness, the State's cross is wide. The "narrow scope" strategy described in `witness-types.md` § 1.3 is harder to execute in Louisiana state court than in federal court. Plan accordingly.

**Art. 611(C) — Leading questions:** Leading questions should NOT be used on direct examination except as necessary to develop the witness's testimony. Ordinarily, leading questions ARE permitted on:
- (1) Cross-examination
- (2) Preliminary matters
- (3) Hostile witnesses
- (4) Adverse parties
- (5) Witnesses identified with adverse parties
- (6) Witnesses whose recollection has been exhausted (refreshing recollection)
- (7) Witnesses with communication or comprehension difficulty (children, mentally impaired)

> **Defense implication:** Defense direct exam of a defense witness uses open-ended questions. Auto-flag leading questions and rephrase. The exceptions are narrow and must be invoked on the record.

**Art. 611(D) — Redirect and recross:** A witness who has been cross-examined may be reexamined as to all matters covered on cross. Recross is permitted on matters covered on redirect. Court may permit additional examination.

### La. C.E. Art. 612 — Writing used to refresh memory

If a witness uses a writing to refresh memory while testifying, the adverse party is entitled to (1) have the writing produced, (2) inspect it, (3) cross-examine on it, (4) introduce relevant portions.

> **Defense implication:** Direct-exam refreshing-recollection technique:
> 1. Establish witness has exhausted memory ("Do you recall the exact time?" — "Not exactly.")
> 2. Show witness the document ("I'm showing you what's marked as DEF-X. Take a moment.")
> 3. Witness reviews silently — does NOT read aloud
> 4. Retrieve document ("Has that refreshed your recollection?")
> 5. Ask the question again — witness now answers from refreshed memory, not from the document
> The document itself does NOT come into evidence merely by use for refreshing. Foundation for substantive admission requires separate predicate.

### La. C.E. Art. 613 — Foundation for prior inconsistent statements

(Relevant to direct exam where defense witness has a prior inconsistent statement the State will use on cross. Get-out-in-front strategy.)

A prior statement of a witness, whether inconsistent or otherwise, is admissible only after the witness has been afforded the opportunity to admit or deny the statement. Where the statement is offered for impeachment, the proper foundation requires direction of the witness's attention to the time, place, and circumstances of the prior statement.

> **Defense implication:** On direct, if a defense witness has a prior inconsistent statement the State will impeach with, address it on direct: identify the prior statement, explain the inconsistency, sanitize the impeachment value. Better the defense raise it than the State.

### La. C.E. Art. 607 — Who may impeach

Any party may attack the credibility of any witness, including a witness called by that party. Defense direct exam may include "get out in front of it" sanitization of a defense witness's own credibility issues.

### La. C.E. art. 608 — Character and conduct of a witness

**⚠ Art. 608(B) bars specific-acts credibility attacks** — "particular acts, vices, or courses of conduct… may not be inquired into or proved by extrinsic evidence" to attack truthfulness, other than convictions under arts. 609/609.1 or as constitutionally required. This is the **opposite** of FRE 608(b). On direct it constrains character-witness examination and what your own witness may be asked to volunteer; on the cross that follows, it constrains the State the same way — flag it when the State strays. Four lawful routes for credibility material: conviction (609.1), bias (607(D)(1)), accuracy of the testimony (607(C)), prior inconsistent statement (613).

(A) Character evidence of witness for truthfulness: opinion or reputation form only, after witness's credibility has been attacked.
(B) Specific instances of conduct: NOT admissible to attack or support credibility through extrinsic evidence (exceptions: criminal convictions under Art. 609; cross-examination of witness directly).

### La. C.E. art. 609.1 — Impeachment by conviction, CRIMINAL cases

**Use art. 609.1, not art. 609.** Art. 609 is the civil rule; art. 609.1 governs criminal cases. Art. 609.1(A) subjects every testifying witness to examination on his convictions; (B) bars inquiry into arrests, warrants, indictments, prosecutions, and acquittals; (C) opens the *details* only on three triggers (denial, exculpatory explanation, or probative value outweighing prejudice); (D) a pending appeal goes to weight, not admissibility; (E) a pardon based on innocence bars it; (F) juvenile adjudications generally inadmissible. **No 10-year limit** — unlike FRE 609(b).

The most heavily litigated impeachment rule in defense direct exam — because the defendant's testifying decision turns on it.

**Art. 609.1 (criminal cases — applicable to defendant who testifies):**

For the purpose of attacking the credibility of a witness in a criminal case, evidence that the witness has been convicted of a crime is admissible only if:
- (1) The witness has been convicted of a crime; AND
- (2) The conviction has not been more than 10 years old (computed from date of release or date of conviction, whichever is later); UNLESS the court determines the probative value, supported by specific facts and circumstances, substantially outweighs the prejudicial effect

**Crimen falsi** (crimes involving dishonesty or false statement) are subject to a separate analysis under Art. 609.1.

**Sanitization motion in limine:** The defense files pretrial to:
- Exclude convictions over 10 years old
- Exclude convictions not crimen falsi
- Sanitize specific offense identification (admit "felony conviction" without naming offense)
- Apply 403 balancing — probative value substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice

> **Defense implication:** Defendant decision matrix (see `defendant-testify-decision-matrix.md`) weighs heavily on 609 outcome. If priors are admissible un-sanitized, this often weighs AGAINST testifying.

**Federal WDLA parallel:** Federal Rule 609 differs:
- Crimen falsi convictions ARE automatically admissible (no balancing) under Rule 609(a)(2)
- Non-crimen-falsi felony convictions: defendant gets reverse balancing (probative outweighs prejudicial) — heightened protection for criminal defendants under Rule 609(a)(1)(B)
- 10-year cutoff with similar exception

### La. C.E. Art. 404(A) — Character evidence; trait-in-issue

(A) Character evidence generally NOT admissible to prove conformity. But:

**(A)(1) Pertinent trait of the accused:** Evidence of a pertinent trait of the accused's character offered by the accused, or by the prosecution to rebut.

> **Defense implication:** This is the rule that authorizes calling character witnesses on the defense case-in-chief. The trait must be PERTINENT to the charge. See `witness-types.md` § 4.1.

**(A)(2) Pertinent trait of the victim:** With limits — see specific sub-rules including Art. 412 (sexual assault victim).

**(A)(3) Character of a witness:** Per Arts. 607, 608, 609.

### La. C.E. Art. 405 — Methods of proving character

(A) Reputation or opinion. In all cases where character evidence is admissible, proof may be by reputation or opinion. Inquiry into specific instances is permitted on cross.

(B) Specific instances of conduct. In cases where character is an essential element of a charge, claim, or defense, proof may also be by specific instances of conduct.

> **Defense implication:** Defense direct of a character witness uses REPUTATION or OPINION form only. Specific instances on direct are prohibited (unless character is an "essential element" — rare in criminal defense; possible in entrapment-style defenses).

### La. C.E. Art. 412 — Sexual assault victim's past behavior

Specific limits on evidence of victim's prior sexual behavior. Relevant to defense direct in sex-offense cases. Procedural prerequisites for admissibility (notice, in camera hearing). Not the focus of this skill but flag for relevance.

### La. C.E. Art. 702 — Testimony by experts

A witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise if (1) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data, (2) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods, and (3) the witness has applied the principles and methods reliably to the facts.

> Tracks Federal Rule 702 (Daubert codification). Louisiana adopted Daubert in State v. Foret, 628 So. 2d 1116 (La. 1993).

### La. C.E. Art. 703 — Bases of expert opinion

Facts or data on which an expert bases an opinion may be those perceived by or made known to the expert at or before the hearing. If of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field, the facts or data need not be admissible in evidence.

> **Defense implication:** Defense experts may rely on otherwise-inadmissible material (e.g., police reports the defense expert reviewed) if the type is reasonably relied upon. The underlying material may be disclosed to the jury only if the court determines probative value substantially outweighs prejudice.

### La. C.E. Art. 704 — Opinion on ultimate issue

Testimony in the form of an opinion or inference otherwise admissible is NOT objectionable because it embraces an ultimate issue. (EXCEPT: in criminal cases, an expert MAY NOT express an opinion as to the guilt or innocence of the accused.)

> **Defense implication:** Defense expert may opine on mental state, causation, identification reliability — but NOT on guilt or innocence directly. Phrase carefully.

### La. C.E. Art. 705 — Disclosure of facts or data underlying expert opinion

An expert may testify in terms of opinion or inference and give reasons therefor WITHOUT first testifying to the underlying facts or data, unless the court requires otherwise. The expert may, in any event, be required to disclose the underlying facts or data on cross-examination.

> **Defense implication:** This is **La. C.E. art. 705** — disclosure of the facts or data underlying an expert opinion. Do not confuse it with La. C.Cr.P. art. 705, which is *"Effects of severance of indictments"* and has nothing to do with experts. The pretrial defense-expert disclosure obligation lives in the La. C.Cr.P. arts. 716–729 discovery scheme `[VERIFY the current article]`.

### La. C.E. Art. 803(6) — Business records hearsay exception

Records of regularly conducted business activity. See `witness-types.md` § 5.2 for the four-prong direct-exam predicate.

### La. C.E. Art. 901-902 — Authentication and self-authentication

Art. 901(A) — General requirement satisfied by evidence sufficient to support a finding that the matter in question is what its proponent claims.

Art. 902 — Self-authentication categories (no extrinsic evidence required):
- Domestic public documents under seal
- Certified copies of public records
- Official publications
- Newspapers and periodicals
- Trade inscriptions
- Acknowledged documents
- Commercial paper
- Certified records of regularly conducted activity (Art. 902(11)) — the business-records self-authentication rule
- Certified records generated by electronic process (Art. 902(13)-(14) — recent additions for digital evidence)

> **Defense implication:** Always assess self-authentication first before calling a foundation witness.

## Part II — Louisiana Code of Criminal Procedure

### Defense expert disclosure — `[VERIFY correct article before citing]`

**⚠ Correction:** a prior version of this file cited **La. C.Cr.P. art. 705** for defense expert disclosure. Art. 705 is *"Effects of severance of indictments"* and has nothing to do with expert disclosure. Defense reciprocal discovery runs through the La. C.Cr.P. arts. 716–729 scheme (art. 719 covers reports of examinations and tests; art. 724 covers documents and tangible objects), substantially restructured by Acts 2013, No. 250. **Confirm the current article and its text before relying on it.** Note also: La. C.E. art. 705 is a different rule entirely — disclosure of the facts or data underlying an expert opinion.

Upon request by the State, the defense shall disclose:
- Names and addresses of expert witnesses defense intends to call
- The expert's qualifications
- The subject matter of the expert's testimony
- The substance of the expert's opinion

Continuing duty to supplement. Sanctions for non-disclosure include exclusion of testimony.

> **Defense implication:** Pre-trial procedural compliance is gate-keeping. See `witness-types.md` § 3.1.

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 727 — Notice of alibi

Upon written demand of the State setting forth the time, date, and place of the alleged offense, the defendant shall serve within the time set by the court a written notice of intention to offer alibi defense, stating:
- The specific place(s) the defendant claims to have been at the time of the offense
- Names and addresses of witnesses upon whom the defendant intends to rely

Art. 727(B) — State's reciprocal disclosure of rebuttal witnesses.

Art. 727(C) — Continuing duty to disclose.

Art. 727(D) — Court may exclude testimony of any undisclosed witness, with limited good-cause exception.

> **Defense implication:** Procedural exclusion is the most common reason an alibi defense fails. See `witness-types.md` § 2.1.

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 731 et seq. — Subpoenas

Defense right to compulsory process. Subpoena issued by the clerk on defense request. Service requirements. Right to attachment for non-appearance.

### La. C.Cr.P. Art. 761 — Order of trial

Order: State opening, defense opening (or reserved), State case-in-chief, defense case (motion for directed verdict permitted before defense rests), State rebuttal, defense surrebuttal (with leave), closing arguments, jury charge.

> **Defense implication:** The defense case fits between the State's resting and the State's rebuttal. Plan witness sequencing accordingly. See `witness-types.md` sequencing default.

## Part III — Constitutional & Federal Doctrine

### Right to testify — Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S. 44 (1987)

The defendant has a constitutional right to testify in his own defense. The right is rooted in the 5th, 6th, and 14th Amendments. The decision to testify is the defendant's, after consultation with counsel.

> **Defense implication:** Counsel advises on testify-or-not. The decision belongs to the defendant. Document the decision in writing.

### Defendant's right to silence — Griffin v. California, 380 U.S. 609 (1965)

The State may NOT comment on the defendant's failure to testify. Jury instructions on the right to silence are required if requested.

> **Defense implication:** Going into trial, both options (testify / not testify) are constitutionally protected. The decision is strategic, not legal.

### Defendant's right to determine when to testify — Brooks v. Tennessee, 406 U.S. 605 (1972)

Tennessee statute requiring defendant to testify first if at all (before other defense witnesses) held unconstitutional. The defendant has the right to decide WHETHER and WHEN to testify, including after hearing other defense witnesses.

> **Defense implication:** Standard sequencing puts the defendant last in the defense case so the defendant can hear all other defense witnesses first. Confirm sequencing pre-trial.

### Estelle v. Williams — 425 U.S. 501 (1976)

Compelling a defendant to stand trial in identifiable prison clothing violates the 14th Amendment. By extension, visible restraints in jury presence are prohibited absent specific justification (Deck v. Missouri, 544 U.S. 622 (2005)).

> **Defense implication:** Civilian clothing for the defendant on the stand. No visible restraints. Court personnel must not refer to defendant by jail ID in jury presence. Document any violation on the record.

### Subject-matter waiver doctrine

When the defendant testifies on direct, the 5th Amendment is waived as to the subject matter of the direct testimony. The State may cross-examine on any subject within the scope of direct, plus credibility. (Mitchell v. United States, 526 U.S. 314 (1999), addressed the parallel sentencing-phase waiver.)

In Louisiana state court, "scope of cross" is wide-open under La. C.E. Art. 611(B) — the practical waiver may be broader than in federal court.

> **Defense implication:** Narrow direct controls cross less in Louisiana than in federal court. See § La. C.E. Art. 611(B) above.

### Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals — 509 U.S. 579 (1993); State v. Foret, 628 So. 2d 1116 (La. 1993)

Trial court gatekeeping role for expert testimony. Five non-exclusive factors:
1. Testability of the methodology
2. Peer review and publication
3. Known or potential error rate
4. Standards controlling operation
5. General acceptance in the relevant scientific community

> **Defense implication:** Defense experts must survive Daubert/Foret. See `witness-types.md` § 3.5.

### Michelson v. United States — 335 U.S. 469 (1948)

Once defendant opens the door to character with a character witness, the State may cross-examine the character witness with "have you heard" or "did you know" questions about specific bad acts of the defendant — provided the State has a good-faith basis.

> **Defense implication:** The open-door risk. See `witness-types.md` § 4.4.

### Deck v. Missouri — 544 U.S. 622 (2005)

Visible shackling during the guilt phase of a criminal trial violates the 14th Amendment absent specific findings of necessity. Extends Estelle v. Williams.

> **Defense implication:** Make the record if the defendant is shackled in jury presence. Object on the record. Preserve appellate issue.

### Federal Rule 16 (defense expert disclosure)

(b)(1)(C) — On government request, defense must produce a written summary of expert testimony defense intends to use in its case-in-chief. Summary must describe (a) the witness's opinions, (b) the bases for those opinions, (c) the witness's qualifications.

> **Federal practice note:** Fed. R. Crim. P. 16 disclosure differs from the Louisiana scheme — sequence and triggers vary, and Rule 16(b)(1)(C) requires an expert summary Louisiana has no direct analogue for. For W.D./E.D./M.D. La. cases, consult the local rules and the scheduling order.

## Part IV — Practical Cross-References

### Get-out-in-front decision tree

Defense witness has a credibility problem (prior conviction, prior inconsistent statement, bias, motive to fabricate, criminal record). The State will use it on cross. Two options:

**Option A — Get out in front:** Address on direct. Witness owns the issue. Jury hears it from a friendly source. Minimizes State's impeachment value.

**Option B — Move in limine to exclude:** Pretrial motion to keep the issue out. If granted, problem solved. If denied, must get out in front anyway — worst of both worlds if missed.

**Decision rule:**
- File the motion in limine if there is a colorable legal basis (Art. 609 time bar, 403 prejudice, sanitization argument)
- Plan the get-out-in-front chapter as a backup in case the motion is denied
- Brief the witness on both scenarios

### Scope discipline strategic posture

| Posture | Description | When to use |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| **Narrow** | Witness testifies to discrete topic only | Defendant with multiple impeachment issues; expert with limited scope; foundation witness |
| **Medium** | Witness testifies to the key event and surrounding context | Alibi witness; eyewitness corroboration |
| **Broad** | Witness testifies to the entire State narrative | Defendant in cases where jury must hear full account; cases where narrow scope reads as evasive |

Document the chosen posture in the STEP 2 pre-draft confirmation.

### Rehearsal vs. coaching ethics line

| Permissible (rehearsal) | Impermissible (coaching) |
|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Reviewing the witness's prior statements with them | Telling the witness what to say |
| Practicing the questions in advance | Suggesting an answer the witness would not otherwise give |
| Mock cross to identify weaknesses | Inventing facts the witness then adopts |
| Demeanor coaching (pace, tone, eye contact) | Scripting verbatim responses |
| Reviewing exhibits the witness will see | Concealing exhibits to manipulate testimony |
| Discussing scope and what to expect | Coaching the witness to lie about scope of conversations with counsel |

Document rehearsal sessions in a memo to file (privileged work product). Never document scripted testimony.
